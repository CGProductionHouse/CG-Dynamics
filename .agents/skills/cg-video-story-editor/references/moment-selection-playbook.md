# Selecting the moment, not just the clip

Use this when a source contains a promising gesture, expression, action, or camera move but only a small part belongs in the story. A contact sheet finds candidates; it cannot establish how the moment feels in motion.

## Give the shot a job

Write the one thing the viewer should understand or feel at this point: an order becomes real, a worker notices a detail, a person welcomes someone, a product is handled. Name what this shot proves and what it must not imply. Genuine staff footage does not by itself identify a current adviser or a customer. Stock business footage does not become a client case.

Then choose a visual treatment that supports the job. A quick action can propel a montage; an expression may need a held shot; a location can bridge examples to the client's actual people. Experiment with a cut on action, a match of movement or shape, a sound that leads the next image, or a short pause after density. [Adobe's edit guidance](https://www.adobe.com/creativecloud/video/discover/edit-a-video.html) places emotion and story alongside rhythm; [its match-cut guide](https://www.adobe.com/creativecloud/video/discover/match-cut.html) explains continuity through visual or audio resemblance. These are choices to test against the rendered sequence, not effects to add by quota.

| Film's job | Style worth testing | Moment that makes it work |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Show a product people want to live with | Tactile detail montage followed by a human-use hold | The hand makes contact, then the product's setting becomes readable |
| Make a professional service feel relevant | Concrete buyer question, distinct examples, then real team/location proof | A visible change in work leads into an unposed welcome or active service moment |
| Capture an event or achievement | Anticipation, reaction, then a wider context shot | The response begins; do not cut only to the posed aftermath |
| Explain a process or craft | Cut on hand/tool action, alternate detail and result | The material visibly changes at the cut |
| Carry a spoken testimonial | Let a meaningful phrase or breath lead the reaction shot | Expression and speech support the same point; retain natural pauses |

Select one style hypothesis from the brief and references, then test its opening, midpoint and ending. A film can combine styles if the change marks a real story turn. Reject a visually clever match when the relationship between shots misleads the viewer.

## Find the decisive frames

1. Watch the entire original once, then the candidate region at normal speed in the intended portrait crop. Note whether the action is genuine, staged, interrupted, repeated, or partly outside the frame. If the client identifies a principal person, record that person's role in the shot rather than treating every face as equal.
2. Scrub more densely around **preparation → onset → payoff → release**. For a smile, the payoff may be the moment the expression becomes recognisable, not the biggest static grin. For a task, it may be the instant the hand contacts the object or the work visibly changes. For a camera move, it may be the point the intended subject is revealed.
3. Mark an in-point with enough setup to understand the action. For a mid-action entrance, the first selected frames must already show displacement or a changed pose; a person waiting to move will make even good slow motion feel hesitant. Mark an out-point before the gesture goes slack, the subject poses, the camera wanders, or a new unrelated action begins. Inspect the frames immediately before and after both marks. Record a rejected adjacent range and why it failed.
4. If the source is high frame rate, test a slower presentation only when it makes the intended moment easier to read. Check cadence and the entire moving crop; a prolonged hold can make normal movement look performative.
5. Put the selected range against the preceding and following shots. Ask whether the viewer can read the action once, whether movement carries across the cut, and whether the next shot advances the story. Do not repeat a source or near-identical action to fill a beat.
6. After the moment works visually, adjust its placement against audible music attacks or a sound bridge. An arbitrary beat is not a reason to include the weaker tail of a performance. [Adobe's Premiere music lesson](https://www.adobe.com/learn/premiere-pro/web/add-music-to-travel-videos) describes beat markers, matching action, shot scale and J cuts as editing aids; listen to the final timeline rather than approving a waveform alone.

When a client rejects a posed moment, retain the rejected time range in the ledger. A different range from the same file can be considered if its movement, expression and relationship between people actually change; a shorter version of the same pose should remain rejected. If the client names a principal team member, give their real interaction a meaningful place in the arc, while avoiding an unsupported claim about their current role.

Record a compact selection ledger: `source | orientation/crop | intended job | in/out | original fps → timeline fps/speed | setup/payoff/exit frames | rejected neighbor | rights/identity | sound or cut rationale`. Keep the source and recipe with the editable project. Review the export at phone size, with and without sound. A still strip is evidence of screening, not final motion or listening approval.
