# Masterclass example: cinematic rush and release

Reference: [Yu-Yen's short editing breakdown on TikTok](https://www.tiktok.com/@yuyenwng/video/7631341066553167126), supplied by the CG editor. The video displays moving footage above an annotated edit timeline. Its visible notes call out short cuts at the opening and end, keyframe zooms across clips, and multiple audio events, including risers, whooshes and source-like mechanical/water sounds. The editor's feedback identifies the key emotional feature: after the quick opening, a pause lets the viewer take in the image. Treat these as observations and feedback, not as a licensed asset pack or measured recipe for every edit.

## What to learn

1. **Write a visual sentence.** Short shots should progress through distinct actions or views. If two adjacent flashes say the same thing, remove one.
2. **Build sound in layers.** Use a rising layer to create anticipation, brief accents at selected picture changes, and an impact or release at the turn. Use real source sound where it is truthful. Put each layer on its own track so an editor can rebalance it.
3. **Resolve the rush.** For this reference pattern, build the opening from sound effects before the main song starts: a rising cue under 6–10 distinct, very short shots; selected cut whooshes; one landing impact. Let the impact decay over a meaningful held image. Give the viewer a short quiet window to read the image before copy or music enters. The sound must build toward the release, not begin with the landing.
4. **Enter the song once.** Start the main music with the first story beat after the hold and keep its phrase intact. A song that plays from frame one and is then ducked to fake a pause breaks the structure the editor requested. Set the song's source in point and timeline entry separately from the sound effects.
5. **Animate from the footage.** A small keyframe zoom can sharpen an existing movement; cuts should follow hands, tools or camera direction. Reject a zoom that invents motion or hides the subject in a vertical crop.

6. **Give the release a new reason to watch.** The held shot should contain a tactile or human action, with the first key word and the song entering together after the quiet. A strong verb that matches the next shot makes the opening rush lead into a buyer story instead of becoming an unrelated effects reel.

## Reference-to-cut method

1. Log the reference's *observable* structure: shot count, approximate shot lengths, moment of rising energy, impact frame, held image, music entrance and sound tails. Label anything that cannot be verified from the accessible reference. Do not invent an exact soundtrack or beat map.
2. Build a new visual sentence from the client's own footage and mark the desired cuts on a blank timeline before adding sound. Check that the last rush shot clearly identifies the client or product when that is the desired reveal.
3. Choose licensed cues by listening, not by name alone. Check the asset waveform so its peak meets the intended cut; some effects have a long lead-in. Place each whoosh slightly before its picture cut and allow its tail to cross it. Keep riser, cut accents, impact, natural sound and song on separate tracks.
4. Export the opening audio stems. Verify that the song is silent before its intended entrance, that the riser grows toward the turn, and that the impact decays before the song. Then listen to the full opening through the first story line on headphones and a phone. A waveform check catches placement errors; it cannot judge taste.
5. Compare the rendered opening with the reference side by side for rhythm and emotional shape, then review the complete client film so the opening belongs to the actual story.

## Practice exercise

Make two openings from eight or nine verified shots. In A, cut straight into the story. In B, use a licensed riser and selected whooshes through the rush, resolve on a meaningful image, hold it briefly, then start the main song with the first story beat. Watch both at phone size, once muted and once on headphones. Log which version makes the first product or human moment clearer, where attention dips, and whether the sound transition feels earned. Record source times, effect peak times, hold duration, song entrance and stem levels in the edit manifest. The hold duration comes from the chosen gesture, not this example.

## Rights and transfer

Study the reference's structure and timing. Do not extract its soundtrack, effects or footage into a client deliverable without a confirmed commercial licence. Recreate an original design from cleared library sounds, authorised production audio or new recordings. A user liking the reference is creative direction, not a transfer of its media rights.
