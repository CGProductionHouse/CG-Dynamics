# Graphic transform blur — portable original proof

## 29 October office background runner screen

[Licensed runner/title comparison](../../RUNNER-GRAPHIC-BLUR-SCREEN-20261029.md) applies the same short `RUN` trajectory to a real left-to-right source, comparing sharp, 180° and a protected 90° revision. All three 72-frame exports decode; the first12 frames are exact. Full-size moving frames show 180° smears the letters more than the 90° candidate, while a sampled settled hold returns near the sharp control (small encoded drift remains). The title stays ahead of the runner in inspected frames and fades before she reaches it. This is a silent, landscape, code-rendered source screen; no native Premiere shutter-equivalence, heard beat, physical-phone readability, CG use or universal 90° setting is proved.

The owner's [three-frame zoom reference #27](https://www.tiktok.com/@miracampschool/video/7504014296779590935) shows Transform shutter-angle blur, including a visible 200-degree example. This card tests the underlying **moving-graphic** mechanism, independently of the source-selection failures in the zoom-cut card. It does not reproduce the reference's exact parameters or establish that native blur replaces RSMB on real footage.

**Use:** Choose the actual position/scale animation first. Compare no blur with restrained blur on the same trajectory and timing. Confine blur to movement, provide a fully sharp reading hold, and check fast entry/exit frames at full and phone scale. A large shutter angle can erase a short word before the audience has a chance to read it. Native editor/composition and layer blur controls must both be verified where applicable. Do not blur an entire caption track to hide poor easing or missed beats.

`python render_ab.py` produces three committed two-second 540×960/24 fps silent previews: [no blur](proof/no-blur.mp4), [180-degree candidate](proof/shutter-180.mp4) and [300-degree stress control](proof/shutter-300.mp4). A [phone contact](proof/phone-contact.jpg), [full-size moving frame](proof/motion-full-size.png), [sharp hold](proof/hold-full-size.png) and [evidence JSON](proof/evidence.json) travel with the card so the office agent can inspect the same result after pulling Git.

The word enters on an eased horizontal path, holds, then accelerates out. Each shutter candidate averages temporal coverage over its exposure interval. The first nine-sample render left discrete repeated letter contours at full size and was rejected. The corrected 129-sample coverage accumulation removes the obvious comb-like repeats; it is a bounded graphic test, not a new production renderer. All variants preserve the static smaller line. Frames 14–34 are pixel-identical to the no-blur version **before encoding**, proving the settled word does not retain blur. All three 48-frame H.264 exports decode.

The [actual decoded hold](proof/encoded-hold.png) was also inspected. Across frames 14–34 its mean absolute RGB difference from the no-blur encoded control is about **0.076 on a 0–255 scale**, consistent with small codec differences rather than a retained blurred title. This measurement supports the sharp-hold construction, not a normal-speed comfort verdict.

**Decision:** The 180-degree version is the restrained construction candidate: motion samples show a continuous directional smear, then a clearly sharp word. The 300-degree version has a longer, less readable smear during fast travel and is a stress control, not the default. The contact is a 135×240 proxy per cell, not a physical-phone or normal-speed comfort test. No sound has been added or heard, no client fit is claimed, and native editor reopen/export remains unverified. The useful shared lesson is to test blur against the exact motion and require sharp hold recovery; do not promote one shutter value to all graphics or source footage.
