# Creative Strategy Gate for Social Posters

Last updated: 2026-09-09

## Purpose

No static poster should move into design because “we need another post this month.” The system must first establish why the poster exists, which real person/moment it speaks to, what useful thought or feeling it should create, and what creative mechanism earns attention.

The standard is deliberately closer to strong human advertising craft than generic AI content generation: a clear proposition, a recognisable human truth, distinctive brand signals and an execution that makes the idea easy to notice and remember.

## Required brief before design

Every DP must have a short strategy record before source selection/generation:

```text
Stable production ID
Brand scope / branch
Business objective
Buying situation / real moment
Audience
Audience tension / need / desire
Primary content role
Single-minded proposition
Proof / reason to believe
Desired emotional response
Desired next thought/action
Current month / place relevance
Distinctive brand assets
Source/media requirement
Creative mechanism
Headline candidates
Recent concepts/people/products/mechanisms to avoid
One-sentence reason this poster deserves to exist
```

If the final line cannot be written convincingly, do not design yet.

## The poster-job sentence

Before approving the brief, complete this sentence in plain language:

> This poster is for **[specific audience/moment]**, and after seeing it we want them to **[feel/think/do one thing]** because **[specific client truth/proof]**.

Example structure only:

> This poster is for Bloem drivers whose fuel light appears during a busy day; we want them to remember this stop as easy and familiar because the actual branch is open 24 hours and real staff are there to help.

This sentence is internal strategy, not necessarily consumer-facing copy.

## Content roles

Every poster has one primary job:

- activation / direct response;
- brand building / memory;
- trust / proof;
- product or service education;
- convenience / reminder;
- local relevance / community;
- entertainment / humour;
- seasonal moment;
- recruitment / announcement;
- event attendance;
- reputation / reassurance.

Secondary benefits are allowed. One poster should not attempt to perform five equal jobs.

## Human-truth requirement

Good social advertising should connect to something the audience already recognises.

Look for:

- a familiar inconvenience;
- a small pleasure;
- a routine or ritual;
- a local observation;
- a seasonal behaviour;
- a useful fact;
- a misconception;
- a moment of urgency;
- a recognisable social interaction;
- a specific product craving/problem;
- a proof point the audience actually cares about.

Do not manufacture emotional language merely to sound warm, premium or human.

## Headline gate

A headline is rejected if it is:

- abstract without a concrete meaning;
- interchangeable with another client;
- generic AI language such as `elevate your experience`, `where quality meets...`, `your trusted partner`, `we've got you covered`, or vague claims about being `human`;
- merely describing the photograph;
- a slogan with no relationship to a real audience moment;
- technically true but strategically pointless;
- trying to say too many things;
- clever only after explanation;
- unsupported by the poster's visual/proof;
- too long or too small to work quickly on a phone.

Before accepting a headline, answer:

1. **What is this line doing?**
2. **Why should this audience care now?**
3. **Could a competitor paste its logo underneath unchanged?**
4. **What feeling/thought should happen in the first second?**
5. **Does the visual make the line stronger, or is it decorative?**

If the team has to explain what a headline means, kill it.

## Strong headline behaviours

A headline should ideally do at least one useful job:

- recognise a real audience problem/moment;
- create relevant curiosity;
- state a desirable outcome;
- expose a useful contrast/tension;
- lead with proof;
- make a locally recognisable observation;
- make the audience smile because it feels true;
- turn an offer/service into a meaningful situation;
- use a surprising but defensible fact;
- create an incomplete thought that the visual resolves;
- state the thing a customer would actually say rather than brand-language commentary.

Short is not automatically good. Specific and instantly meaningful is good.

## Human-emotion check

Before copy approval, state the intended result in ordinary speech.

Examples:

- “I know exactly what that feels like.”
- “That is useful; I'll remember this.”
- “That made me hungry.”
- “That is clever.”
- “I trust these people.”
- “That solves an annoying little problem.”
- “That feels like Bloem, not national stock copy.”
- “I want to show this to someone.”
- “I didn't know that.”
- “That offer is actually relevant to me.”

If the intended response is only “this sounds premium,” the brief is not strong enough.

## Copy-to-visual dependency

The words and visual should need each other where possible.

Avoid a workflow where:

- headline is generic;
- photo is generic;
- logo is the only client-specific element.

Prefer executions where the real photo, product, place, person, offer or proof creates the reason the headline works.

The goal is not to decorate copy. The goal is one joined advertising idea.

## Creative-mechanism rule

Select the design mechanism after strategy, not before it.

Possible mechanisms include:

- photographic editorial cover;
- cutout subject breaking the grid;
- dynamic gradient/light treatment;
- oversized type interacting with subject;
- product macro / scale play;
- sticker / handwritten annotation;
- bold price/offer architecture;
- visual contradiction;
- before/after or comparison;
- map/location idea;
- magazine/newspaper-inspired advert;
- retro local advertising reference;
- collage;
- photographic sequence;
- graphic iconography;
- typographic poster;
- cropped-detail teaser;
- visual metaphor grounded in client truth;
- restrained premium minimalism when the message genuinely benefits from it.

A mechanism is not a template. It is the visual idea used to deliver this poster's strategy.

## Reference/research rule

When useful, research adjacent industries and current advertising/design behaviour for creative stimulation. Do not merely search for “fuel station posters” and copy the category.

Useful reference sources can include:

- historic magazine/newspaper advertising;
- current outdoor advertising;
- packaging and retail campaigns;
- editorial/magazine design;
- food/fashion/automotive campaigns;
- local South African cultural/seasonal cues;
- current platform-native visual trends.

Extract principles/mechanisms, not layouts, taglines or protected campaign assets to reproduce.

Client Canva history has a different job: it defines the client's existing visual grammar and quality continuity. External research should add freshness without redesigning the brand.

## Monthly marketing-mix rule

Before creating a month, review the previous month and deliberately decide the next mix.

For each planned DP note:

- primary content role;
- audience/moment;
- subject/product/service/branch;
- light/dark visual weight;
- headline position/type behaviour;
- photo treatment;
- creative mechanism;
- intended emotional result.

Then inspect the batch for collisions.

A month fails the mix test if three posters all:

- use the same person;
- promote the same service without a strategic reason;
- place the headline in the same area;
- use the same dark gradient/cutout mechanism;
- rely on the same offer architecture;
- repeat the previous month's strongest-looking poster because it was easy.

## Three layers of novelty

Freshness should exist across three independent layers:

1. **Message novelty** — a new useful angle, situation, fact or reason to care.
2. **Media novelty** — a fresh photo/person/product/branch or a justified historic reuse.
3. **Execution novelty** — a different art-direction mechanism/layout rhythm.

A poster with only one fresh layer is often still repetitive.

## Mobile-first QA

Review at realistic phone size, not only zoomed desktop size.

- hook/headline is legible almost immediately;
- essential information does not depend on tiny footer copy;
- the focal point survives feed context/crop;
- the viewer can understand hierarchy before reading every word;
- logo/brand attribution is clear without dominating every composition;
- decorative effects increase attention/hierarchy rather than noise;
- body copy is used only when the audience has a reason to read it.

## Pre-generation stop/go gate

A DP can enter visual generation only if all are true:

- exact Dynamics deliverable resolved;
- brand scope resolved;
- one primary job selected;
- audience/buying moment is specific enough;
- proposition is useful and defensible;
- proof/source exists;
- intended feeling/action is explicit;
- headline direction passes the headline gate;
- source media passes freshness review;
- creative mechanism is materially different from recent neighbouring posts;
- the concept still makes sense at phone-feed speed.

If not, return to strategy. Do not try to rescue a weak idea with effects.

## Post-generation strategy QA

After the design exists, ask again:

- can a stranger explain what this poster is about in two seconds?
- does the design actually deliver the intended feeling?
- does the photo/visual prove or sharpen the copy?
- is the client visible through distinctive assets, not generic style alone?
- would this still be worth publishing if the package did not require a DP slot?

If the answer to the last question is no, the poster should normally be reworked.

## Evidence and learning

Monthly strategy should eventually incorporate Dynamics approval/performance evidence, including:

- content the client asked to boost;
- comments/saves/shares where available;
- meaningful audience responses;
- paid-support/spend context;
- client-requested repeats;
- rejected concepts and reasons;
- direct enquiries or other verified outcomes;
- creative mechanism/headline/source used;
- branch/person/product representation.

Do not optimise solely for likes/views. Performance must be interpreted against the poster's primary job and broader brand-building role.
