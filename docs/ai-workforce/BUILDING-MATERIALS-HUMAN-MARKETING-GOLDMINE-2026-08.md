# Building Materials, Construction Supply and Home Improvement — Human Marketing Goldmine

Last updated: 2026-08-06
Status: Human-led marketing research pack; review-gated.
Territory: South Africa first, with transferable international campaign evidence.

## Purpose

Help CG Production House market:

- building-material suppliers;
- hardware and home-improvement retailers;
- timber, steel, roofing, paint, waterproofing, stone, concrete and related product businesses;
- contractor-facing and homeowner-facing suppliers;
- renovation and project brands.

This pack is only about marketing, advertising, SEO, content, promotions, events, customer trust and lead generation.

It excludes bookkeeping, tax, procurement administration and generic construction operations.

## Core human truth

People do not buy building products because the brand says “quality you can trust”.

They buy because they are afraid of:

- wasting money;
- choosing the wrong product;
- being misled by a contractor or salesperson;
- buying too little or too much;
- delaying a project;
- ending with a result that looks cheap;
- having to redo work later;
- not knowing what questions to ask.

Useful marketing must reduce uncertainty, make the project feel achievable and show proof in a way ordinary people and tradespeople can use.

## Proven campaign lessons

### James Hardie — Changing the Way Australia Builds

Source: WARC Awards for Effectiveness.

Documented results:

- 14% increase in brand preference among female homeowners;
- 4,128 marketing-qualified leads;
- 69% conversion rate.

Human insight:

Women were often the renovation visionaries but felt overlooked or dismissed by the building category.

Why it worked:

- spoke to a real emotional frustration;
- treated homeowners as active decision-makers;
- connected inspiration with practical product choice;
- used TV, native content, social and search together.

Transferable lesson:

Do not market only to contractors. Homeowners, partners and family members often shape the brief, look and final choice.

### UltraTech Cement — Baat Ghar Ki

Source: WARC.

Documented results across reported phases:

- 72% increase in brand recall;
- 38% increase in recommendations;
- 44% of homebuilders identified the brand as most reliable in one reported campaign;
- earlier campaign reporting included 200% growth in brand searches and a 15% lift in recommendation.

Human insight:

Individual homebuilders often rely on contractors, relatives and word of mouth because they lack a clear source of trusted building guidance.

Why it worked:

- useful education instead of repetitive strength claims;
- relatable hosts and technical experts;
- local-language and radio reach;
- content followed the full home-building journey.

Transferable lesson:

Become the helpful guide people consult before they arrive at the till.

### UltraTech Cement — One Home, One Chance

Source: WARC.

Documented reporting:

- campaign reached hundreds of millions;
- increased brand recall, recommendation and reported sales/profit outcomes.

Human insight:

For many families, building a home is a once-in-a-lifetime decision. Choosing badly feels irreversible.

Why it worked:

It moved away from generic “strong cement” claims and connected the product to the emotional weight of building a home once and building it properly.

Transferable lesson:

Sell the consequence of a good choice, not only the product feature.

### B&Q — Inspiring Home Improvements

Source: IPA effectiveness case via WARC.

Documented result:

- the long-term 4Homes sponsorship contributed £233 million in incremental sales over three years.

Human insight:

People often need confidence and inspiration before they need a product list.

Why it worked:

- built sustained presence around home and property content;
- met people while they were imagining improvements;
- made the brand part of the project journey, not only the purchase moment.

Transferable lesson:

Own a repeatable content territory such as “fix it properly”, “weekend project”, “before you build”, or “ask the expert”.

### Home Depot — Local mobile intent

Source: Think with Google.

Documented result:

- more than 8x in-store ROI from mobile display using location extensions;
- 93% of ad-attributed sales happened in store.

Human insight:

A person searching for a product near a store is often very close to action.

Transferable lesson:

Use local inventory, branch location, directions, call buttons and exact product availability to turn online demand into foot traffic.

### Home Depot Québec — Local relevance

Source: WARC / Canadian Advertising Success Stories.

Documented outcomes:

- increased store visits;
- increased market share;
- improved brand consideration.

Human insight:

Customers felt the brand looked foreign and generic. Local talent, local products and guidance made it feel more relevant.

Transferable lesson:

A national or franchise brand still needs local people, language, places, stock and project examples.

## Buyer groups and what they care about

### Homeowner or renovator

Wants:

- inspiration;
- plain-language advice;
- price confidence;
- help choosing quantities and finishes;
- reassurance they are not making an expensive mistake;
- examples that look achievable.

Useful content:

- before/after projects;
- “what this really costs” explainers;
- product comparisons;
- finish and colour guides;
- room-by-room project planning;
- mistakes to avoid;
- real customer stories.

### Contractor, builder or installer

Wants:

- reliable stock;
- fast collection or delivery;
- technical confidence;
- trade pricing or account value;
- staff who understand the job;
- fewer delays and callbacks.

Useful content:

- product performance demonstrations;
- application tips;
- “job saved” stories;
- stock alerts;
- contractor spotlights;
- time-saving comparisons;
- trade mornings and supplier demos.

### Architect, designer or specifier

Wants:

- samples;
- finish quality;
- technical data;
- consistency;
- credible project references;
- responsive support.

Useful content:

- material detail photography;
- case studies;
- specification guides;
- sample-box campaigns;
- CPD-style educational sessions where legitimate;
- project collaborations.

### DIY customer

Wants:

- confidence;
- a clear list;
- simple steps;
- realistic difficulty level;
- help correcting mistakes;
- reassurance without being spoken down to.

Useful content:

- one-minute demonstrations;
- “buy these five things” checklists;
- staff-led tutorials;
- weekend challenges;
- beginner mistakes;
- tool and material bundles.

## Messaging angles that feel human

Use:

- “Do it once. Do it properly.”
- “Before you buy, check this.”
- “The mistake that costs more later.”
- “What your contractor should explain before the job starts.”
- “What this finish actually looks like in a real home.”
- “The right product for this exact problem.”
- “What you need for one room / one wall / one weekend.”
- “Built by local hands, for local homes.”

Avoid:

- “Building dreams together.”
- “Where quality meets innovation.”
- “Your trusted partner in excellence.”
- “Passion meets purpose.”
- “Solutions for every need.”

## Human commercial script structures

### The expensive mistake

1. Open on a familiar failure: peeling paint, leaking roof, cracked grout, warped board.
2. Let the homeowner or contractor say what they thought would happen.
3. Show the real cause in plain language.
4. Demonstrate the correct product or method.
5. End with the cost or frustration avoided.

### The project decision

1. Person stands between two real choices.
2. They ask the question customers actually ask.
3. Staff member explains the trade-off without overselling.
4. Show both options in use.
5. Give a simple decision rule.

### The site save

1. Real site pressure: wrong quantity, weather change, urgent replacement, missing product.
2. Show how the supplier responded.
3. Feature the contractor or customer, not only the brand.
4. End with the job moving again.

### The one-home insight

1. Start with the emotional importance of the project.
2. Show one irreversible decision.
3. Explain why the product matters.
4. Close on the finished human outcome: a room used, family moving in, business opening.

## Photography direction

Prioritise:

- hands using the product;
- real texture and material detail;
- product in an actual finished project;
- staff helping a real customer;
- contractor portraits on site;
- delivery moments;
- before/after framing from the same angle;
- imperfect real environments rather than only polished catalogue shots;
- scale references so customers understand dimensions.

Avoid:

- empty product shelves;
- random forklift photos with no story;
- fake handshakes;
- stock images of hard hats;
- generic building silhouettes;
- over-edited “luxury” visuals that misrepresent ordinary products.

## Review and reputation strategy

Ask for reviews after:

- successful delivery;
- quote support;
- product problem solved;
- contractor account setup;
- project completion;
- staff member provided useful advice.

Prompt for useful detail:

- what project was it;
- what problem did the team solve;
- was stock available;
- was delivery reliable;
- did the advice help;
- would they return.

Do not script fake praise or pressure customers into five-star reviews.

## Books and human sources

Useful human sources for the team:

- Why We Buy — Paco Underhill: observation of real shopping behaviour;
- The Choice Factory — Richard Shotton: behavioural patterns affecting purchase decisions;
- Building a StoryBrand — Donald Miller: clarity and customer-centred messaging, used selectively and without formulaic copy;
- Ogilvy on Advertising — David Ogilvy: proof, specificity and direct-response discipline;
- Hey, Whipple, Squeeze This — Luke Sullivan: human creative craft and avoiding ad clichés;
- interviews with contractors, homeowners, architects, installers and counter staff;
- customer reviews and call transcripts;
- real search-query reports;
- sales-team objection logs;
- site visits and product demonstrations.

Books are idea sources, not automatic truth. Industry-specific interviews and observed customer behaviour outrank generic frameworks.

## Review-state Skill Cards

### BUILDING-MATERIALS-HUMAN-CONTENT-BRIEF

Inputs:

- audience;
- exact product or project;
- human problem;
- proof available;
- format;
- CTA.

Output:

- one human insight;
- one useful promise;
- one proof point;
- one real scene;
- one clear action.

Reject the brief when it relies on generic quality claims with no proof.

### BUILDING-MATERIALS-PROJECT-CAMPAIGN

Steps:

1. Choose one real project type.
2. Map the questions from idea to completion.
3. Build content around each decision stage.
4. Connect search, social, video, branch and sales follow-up.
5. Use real people and actual materials.
6. Measure quote requests, store visits, calls, samples, leads and completed sales where verified.

### BUILDING-MATERIALS-DEMO-ACTIVATION

Steps:

1. Pick one visible problem.
2. Demonstrate the product under realistic conditions.
3. Film the customer questions.
4. Capture proof and limitations.
5. Link the event to ads, retargeting, sales follow-up and reusable content.

## Anti-sludge rule

Reject any line that could be pasted onto a law firm, restaurant, car dealer or gym without changing the meaning.

The work must sound like people who have stood in a half-finished room, waited for materials, argued over finishes, fixed a leak, carried boards, measured twice and paid for mistakes.
