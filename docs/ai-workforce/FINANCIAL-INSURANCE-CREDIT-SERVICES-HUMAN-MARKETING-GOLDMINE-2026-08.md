# Financial, Insurance and Credit Services Human Marketing Goldmine

Last updated: 2026-08-06
Status: Researched industry pack. All derived knowledge and skill-card candidates remain review-gated.
Scope: Marketing, customer education, lead generation, trust, regulated claims, conversion and reporting for banks, insurers, advisers, lenders, credit providers, brokers and related financial-service brands.
Excludes: General bookkeeping, tax administration, internal finance operations and generic legal summaries that do not directly affect marketing, customer communication or advertised claims.

## Purpose

This pack helps CG Production House create marketing that earns trust in a high-stakes category.

Financial marketing fails when it hides complexity, overpromises outcomes, pressures vulnerable people or speaks in empty corporate language. Strong work makes risk, cost, eligibility, process and next steps easier to understand without pretending that every product suits everyone.

The central marketing job is not to make finance sound exciting. It is to make the customer feel:

- understood;
- informed;
- safe enough to take the next step;
- clear on what the product does and does not do;
- confident that the provider is legitimate;
- able to compare options without being manipulated.

## Category map

This pack applies to:

- retail and business banking;
- short-term and long-term insurance;
- medical, life, funeral, vehicle, household and business insurance;

- retirement, investments and savings;
- personal loans and responsible credit;

Every campaign must narrow itself to the actual licensed product, audience and decision stage.

## South African regulatory marketing foundation

### Clear, fair and not misleading

The FSCA Conduct Standard for Banks requires financial products and services to be advertised in a way that is clear, fair and not misleading. Advertising must be factually correct, may not contain fraudulent, untrue or misleading statements, and retail-facing material must use plain language.

Marketing implications:

- the headline, visual, voiceover, caption, landing page and disclaimer must describe the same offer;
- critical restrictions may not be buried where a reasonable customer will miss them;
- comparisons must use like-for-like terms and current evidence;
- a marketer or agency acting for a regulated provider does not remove the provider's responsibility;
- customer suitability and target audience matter before media targeting begins.

Canonical source:

- https://lawlibrary.org.za/akn/za/act/standard/fsca/2020/3/eng@2020-07-03

### Treating Customers Fairly

The FSCA's Treating Customers Fairly framework expects customers to receive clear information before, during and after sale; suitable advice where advice is provided; products and service that perform as led to expect; and no unreasonable barriers when changing products, claiming or complaining.

Marketing implications:

- the campaign promise must survive the claims and service experience;
- customer-service friction can destroy paid-media efficiency and reputation;
- claims, cancellation, complaint and support content belong in the marketing journey;
- a product should not be marketed as effortless if the actual process is difficult or exclusion-heavy.

Canonical source:

- https://www.fsca.co.za/Financial-Consumer/

### Performance, forecasts and guarantees

FAIS-related advertising rules require performance data, awards and rankings to identify source and date. Illustrations, forecasts and hypothetical returns need clear assumptions and must be identified as non-guaranteed where applicable.

Marketing implications:

- never crop a return chart to exaggerate performance;
- never turn an illustrative future value into a promise;
- always preserve time period, fees, risk, assumptions and source context;
- old awards are not permanent proof of current quality;
- one customer's saving or claim outcome is not automatically typical.

Canonical source:

- https://www.acts.co.za/financial/bn102_10__advertising

### Licensing and legitimacy

Customers can use FSCA resources to verify whether a provider is authorised. Licence and registration proof can be an important trust signal, but it must be accurate and relevant to the exact entity and service advertised.

Canonical sources:

- https://www.fsca.co.za/
- https://www.fsca.co.za/Financial-Consumer/

## Human buyer truths

### 1. People fear hidden costs more than they dislike complexity

Customers can accept a complex product when the cost, exclusions and trade-offs are explained honestly. They react badly when the real cost only appears after they have invested time or shared personal information.

Useful content:

- what the premium or repayment includes;
- what changes the price;
- common exclusions;
- fees explained in rands and percentages;
- what happens when circumstances change;
- worked examples with labelled assumptions.

### 2. People want to know whether they will be helped when things go wrong

Insurance and advice are judged during claims, emergencies, market declines and financial stress. Marketing should show the service process, not only the purchase moment.

Useful content:

- what happens after a claim is submitted;
- who answers the phone;
- what documents are usually needed;
- typical process stages without promising a fixed approval time;
- real support staff explaining common obstacles;
- complaint and escalation routes.

### 3. Customers compare legitimacy before product detail

Before reading rates or benefits, many customers ask:

- Is this company real?
- Is it licensed?
- Will they disappear with my money?
- Can I speak to a person?
- What do current customers say?
- Are the contact details and offices genuine?

Marketing must make proof easy to find.

### 4. Financial shame delays action

Debt, poor credit, underinsurance and weak savings often carry shame. Humiliating or fear-heavy creative may produce clicks but can reduce trust and lead quality.

Stronger language:

- “Let's look at where you are now.”
- “Start with the numbers you have.”
- “See what may be realistic for your budget.”
- “Understand the options before you decide.”

Avoid:

- “Only irresponsible people have no cover.”
- “Your family will suffer because you failed to plan.”
- “Fix your bad financial habits now.”

### 5. People need the decision broken into smaller steps

A high-consideration financial conversion may involve:

1. recognising a need;
2. understanding the product category;
3. checking legitimacy;
4. estimating affordability or suitability;
5. comparing providers;
6. speaking to a person;
7. submitting information;
8. receiving terms or advice;
9. accepting or declining;
10. experiencing service after sale.

Marketing should identify the exact step it is trying to move.

## Human-source research programme

Before writing an industry campaign, collect language from:

- customer reviews;
- complaint themes;
- claims questions;
- adviser and call-centre interviews;
- sales-call objections;
- search queries;
- quote-form abandonment reasons;
- declined or unsuitable application themes;
- policy wording questions;
- customer-service transcripts where lawful and approved;
- ombud and regulator education material;
- public scam warnings;
- financial literacy workshops.

Do not convert one complaint into an industry-wide truth. Tag the source, date, product and context.

## Messaging territories

### Clarity

Show:

- the real price components;
- who the product may suit;
- what the customer must provide;
- what happens next;
- common exclusions or limitations.

### Protection from a specific disruption

Do not sell vague peace of mind. Show the actual disruption:

- a burst geyser;
- business equipment theft;
- vehicle downtime;
- income interruption;
- funeral costs;
- a hospital cash-flow gap;
- retirement income uncertainty.

Then explain what the product can and cannot address.

### Progress

Useful for saving, investing, debt reduction and SME finance:

- first emergency fund;
- first month of consistent saving;
- paying down a balance;
- improving documentation;
- understanding credit cost;
- preparing for a financial conversation.

Avoid promising wealth, approval or a specific return.

### Human support

Show the actual people behind:

- onboarding;
- advice;
- claims;
- underwriting;
- service;
- complaints;
- business finance.

The strongest proof is specific competence, not staged smiling-at-laptop footage.

## Commercial and short-form script structures

### 1. The cost-explainer script

Hook:

“Why did these two people get different insurance quotes for the same type of car?”

Body:

Explain three to five real rating factors in plain language.

Proof:

Show an example with fictionalised numbers and clearly labelled assumptions.

Close:

“Get the exact terms for your own circumstances before deciding.”

### 2. The claim-process script

Hook:

“You submitted a claim. What happens now?”

Body:

Show the actual process in stages.

Human proof:

A claims specialist explains what commonly delays the process.

Close:

“Your policy terms and claim circumstances still determine the final outcome.”

### 3. The comparison script

Hook:

“Lowest premium and best value are not always the same thing.”

Body:

Compare excess, cover limits, exclusions, service and optional benefits.

Close:

“Compare the full terms, not only the monthly number.”

### 4. The adviser myth script

Hook:

“A financial plan is not a product list.”

Body:

Show the questions a responsible adviser should ask before recommending anything.

Close:

“No recommendation should be made before your circumstances are understood.”

### 5. The responsible-credit script

Hook:

“A lower monthly instalment can still cost more overall.”

Body:

Show term, interest, fees and total repayment using a clearly labelled example.

Close:

“Check the total cost and affordability before accepting credit.”

### 6. The fraud-warning script

Hook:

“Three things a legitimate provider should never ask you to do in a WhatsApp chat.”

Body:

Use current official scam guidance.

Close:

“Verify the provider and contact them through an official channel.”

Content must distinguish general education, product information and personalised advice.

## Website conversion standard

A financial-services landing page should answer:

1. Who is the provider?
2. Is the provider authorised for this service?
3. What is being offered?
4. Who may it suit?
5. What does it cost or how is price determined?
6. What are the important limitations?
7. What happens after form submission?
8. Which personal information is requested and why?
9. Can the customer speak to a person?
10. How can the customer complain or verify legitimacy?

Do not use a long form before giving enough information to judge relevance.

Useful for property, vehicle and business cover. Teach customers what documentation and evidence help establish a claim without implying guaranteed acceptance.

Examples:

- first job;

## Giveaways

Giveaways must support education or preparedness, not pressure people into unsuitable products.

Suitable examples:

- emergency document organiser;
- vehicle safety kit;
- small-business continuity kit;
- financial-literacy workshop seats;
- consultation or needs-assessment session with clear scope;
- budgeting workbook;
- fraud-prevention resources;
- data voucher for a verified online education event.

Avoid:

- “win debt cancellation” without lawful, fully funded and clear terms;
- prizes conditional on taking unsuitable credit;
- collecting excessive personal data;
- making entry look like product approval;
- implying the winner received a typical financial outcome.

## Review and reputation system

Track themes separately:

- quote and onboarding;
- adviser experience;
- claims;
- service response;
- cancellation;
- billing;
- digital platform;
- complaint resolution.

A high average rating can hide severe claims or cancellation problems.

Response standard:

- acknowledge the concern;
- do not disclose private financial information;
- move the case into a secure support channel;
- avoid arguing about policy interpretation publicly;
- close the loop internally;
- use repeated themes to improve product communication.

## Lead quality

A lead is not a sale, approval, policy, funded loan or retained customer.

Track:

- enquiry;
- reachable lead;
- verified identity where applicable;
- suitable product interest;
- completed needs analysis or application;
- quote issued;
- advice completed where applicable;
- approved or declined;
- accepted terms;
- policy active or account funded;
- cooling-off or early cancellation;
- retained customer;
- claim or service experience.

Marketing optimisation should not reward approvals that later cancel, lapse, default or generate unsuitable outcomes.

## Reporting model

Report separately:

- qualified reach;
- education engagement;
- consultation or quote requests;
- reachable leads;
- completed applications;
- suitable opportunities;
- approvals and declines where lawful and useful;
- funded accounts, active policies or completed advice engagements;
- cost per stage;
- cancellation or lapse;
- complaint and review themes;
- retained value;
- attribution limitations.

Do not report:

- quote requests as customers;
- approved credit as healthy long-term revenue;
- application value as paid revenue;
- illustrative returns as achieved results;
- media-platform conversion value as audited financial value.

## Anti-sludge language rules

Reject:

- “your trusted financial partner” without proof;
- “secure your future today”;
- “peace of mind guaranteed”;
- “financial freedom starts here”;
- “we make your dreams a reality”;
- “tailored solutions for every need”;
- “unbeatable rates” without current like-for-like evidence;
- “instant approval” where checks still apply;
- “risk-free investment”;
- “guaranteed returns” unless the exact lawful product terms support it.

Prefer:

- exact customer situation;
- exact product function;
- exact process;
- exact limitation;
- exact proof;
- one clear next step.

## Visual direction

Avoid:

- random piggy banks;
- generic handshakes;
- floating coins;
- fake stock-market charts;
- staged families staring at tablets;
- luxury imagery that implies guaranteed wealth;
- small unreadable disclaimers.

Use:

- real advisers, service and claims staff;
- customer environments with permission;
- legible numbers and worked examples;
- process diagrams;
- real documents with private information removed;
- location and contact proof;
- customer questions on screen;
- calm, credible production.

## Limitations

- Public campaign-performance data for South African financial brands is inconsistent.
- Business growth cannot be attributed to advertising without controlled or well-supported evidence.
- Regulatory requirements vary by exact product, entity, channel and advice status.
- This pack does not replace legal, compliance, actuarial, credit or financial-advice review.
- Customer stories require permission, privacy controls and accurate contextual framing.
- Any product-specific campaign must be checked against current official terms and current regulation before publication.
