## Evidence record 1 — Mere exposure requires meaningful processing

Source:
Yagi, Y. and Inoue, K. (2018), “The Contribution of Attention to the Mere Exposure Effect for Parts of Advertising Images”, Frontiers in Psychology.

Canonical source:
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6134073/

Rights:
CC BY open access.

Research question:
Does repeated exposure to parts of an advertising image increase positive evaluation, and what role does attention play?

Design:
Controlled psychological experiments using repeated exposure to advertising-image components and subsequent affective evaluation.

Finding:
Repeatedly presented stimuli can be evaluated more positively than novel stimuli, but the effect depends on how the exposed material is processed. Exposure alone should not be treated as a guaranteed advertising benefit.

Safe candidate knowledge:
Repeated exposure may improve familiarity or evaluation under some conditions, but attention, recognition, context and execution affect the outcome.

Limitations:
Laboratory exposure to image components is not the same as complete campaign performance, purchase behaviour or long-term brand growth.

Prohibited overclaim:
“More frequency always improves advertising performance.”

## Evidence record 2 — Exposure without awareness may not create the effect

Source:
de Zilva, D., Vu, L., Newell, B. and Pearson, J. (2013), “Exposure Is Not Enough: Suppressing Stimuli from Awareness Can Abolish the Mere Exposure Effect”, PLOS ONE.

Canonical source:
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3798387/

Rights:
CC BY open access.

Design:
Controlled experiments suppressing exposed stimuli from awareness and testing later preference.

Finding:
The mere-exposure effect can disappear when the stimulus is prevented from reaching awareness.

Safe candidate knowledge:
Impressions alone do not prove that advertising was meaningfully processed. Visibility, attention and creative recognition matter.

Limitations:
This is experimental psychology evidence, not a direct platform-media effectiveness benchmark.

Prohibited overclaim:
“Every impression builds brand preference.”

## Evidence record 3 — Repetition can improve recall without changing perceived risk or benefit

Source:
Aikin, K. et al. (2019), “Taking repeated exposure into account: An experimental study of direct-to-consumer prescription drug television ad effects”.

Canonical source:
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9479315/

Rights:
Open-access article; verify article-level reuse terms before ingestion.

Design:
Randomised experiment, n=616. Participants saw a mock television advertisement once, twice or four times within one viewing session.

Finding:
More exposures improved recall and recognition of both benefit and risk information. Exposure frequency was not associated with participants’ estimates of the magnitude or likelihood of those risks and benefits.

Safe candidate knowledge:
Repeated exposure can improve memory for communicated information, but memory improvement is not the same as persuasion, purchase, conversion or correct interpretation.

Limitations:
Single category, controlled viewing session, regulated health-ad context and compressed time period.

Prohibited overclaim:
“Four exposures is the ideal frequency for all campaigns.”

## Evidence record 4 — High exposure creates source-memory risk

Source:
Koch, T. and colleagues (2022), “Coping with high advertising exposure: a source-monitoring perspective”.

Canonical source:
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9444107/

Rights:
CC BY 4.0 open access.

Research focus:
How people distinguish advertising claims and their sources under high advertising exposure.

Safe candidate knowledge:
High exposure environments can create memory and source-attribution problems. Reporting should not treat message recall as proof that the correct brand or claim was remembered.

Limitations:
The exact implications depend on the experimental conditions, category and message similarity.

Prohibited overclaim:
“High frequency always causes brand confusion.”

## Evidence record 5 — Creativity is not only novelty

Source:
Anghelcev, G. and related authors (2014), “Advertising Creativity — The Right Balance between Surprise, Medium and Message Relevance”.

Canonical source:
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2212567114005735

Rights:
Open access under a Creative Commons licence.

Design:
Study of non-traditional advertising formats among university students.

Finding:
Creative effectiveness depended on the relationship between surprise, the chosen medium and relevance to the advertising message.

Safe candidate knowledge:
Creative work should balance novelty with message and medium relevance. Surprise without relevance is not automatically effective.

Limitations:
Student sample and non-traditional-ad context restrict generalisation.

Prohibited overclaim:
“The most surprising creative will perform best.”

## Evidence record 6 — Creativity may support awareness, memory and liking

Source:
Heliyon (2023), “Advertising creativity: Its influence on media response states towards the Hierarchy of Effects”.

Canonical source:
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2405844023064915

DOI:
10.1016/j.heliyon.2023.e19283

Rights:
Open access under a Creative Commons licence.

Design:
Research focused on Filipino millennials in the National Capital Region and interactive digital media.

Finding:
The study linked advertising creativity particularly with awareness, memory and brand liking within its tested hierarchy-of-effects model.

Safe candidate knowledge:
Creative advertising may influence intermediate outcomes such as attention, awareness, memory and brand response. These outcomes should be measured separately from conversion.

Limitations:
Specific geography, age cohort and theoretical model. Does not prove universal sales uplift.

Prohibited overclaim:
“Creative advertising directly causes sales.”

## Evidence record 7 — B2B advertising can benefit from creativity

Source:
“Advertising to businesses: Does creativity matter?”, Industrial Marketing Management, 2016.

Canonical source:
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S001985011500293X

DOI:
10.1016/j.indmarman.2015.10.001

Access:
Metadata/abstract and publisher record. Full-text reuse rights must be verified before ingestion.

Design:
Actual advertisements were shown to managers and creative messaging was assessed against attitudinal and behavioural-intention outcomes.

Finding:
More creative B2B advertisements were associated with stronger attitudes toward the ad and brand and stronger behavioural intentions in the study.

Safe candidate knowledge:
B2B communication should not assume that factual benefit lists alone are sufficient. Creativity can matter alongside credibility, relevance and proof.

Limitations:
Behavioural intention is not verified purchase or revenue. Full methods and sample must be reviewed before activation.

Prohibited overclaim:
“B2B buyers prefer emotional advertising to rational information.”

## Evidence record 8 — B2B creativity can be operationalised as novelty plus relevance

Source:
“Operationalizing ad creativity and its effects in B2B advertising”, Industrial Marketing Management, 2025.

Canonical source:
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0019850125000318

DOI:
10.1016/j.indmarman.2025.02.011

Access:
Publisher abstract/metadata. Do not ingest full text without rights confirmation.

Finding from publisher record:
The research proposes novelty and relevance as measurable dimensions of B2B ad creativity. An eye-tracking experiment found attention and recall effects associated with novel elements.

Safe candidate knowledge:
When reviewing B2B creative, evaluate both novelty and relevance rather than treating unusual execution as sufficient.

Limitations:
The exact scale, samples and effect sizes require full-paper review.

Prohibited overclaim:
“Novel B2B ads always improve commercial performance.”

## Evidence record 9 — B2B advertising effects can be lagged and analytically obscured

Source:
“Advertising in business markets — The obscured bottom-line effect and need for appropriate analytics”, Industrial Marketing Management, 2025.

Canonical source:
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0019850125000239

DOI:
10.1016/j.indmarman.2025.02.003

Rights:
Open access under a Creative Commons licence.

Finding from the publisher record:
The paper argues that B2B advertising can appear unprofitable when lagged effects, capability, competition and compound measures are handled incorrectly.

Safe candidate knowledge:
B2B campaign evaluation may require longer measurement windows and appropriate outcome models. Immediate direct return is not always a complete measure.

Limitations:
This does not permit vague claims that every unprofitable campaign will work later.

Prohibited overclaim:
“Poor short-term B2B results should be ignored because brand effects are delayed.”

## Evidence record 10 — CTR and CVR answer different questions

Source:
“Subjective or objective: How the style of text in computational advertising influences consumer behaviors?”, Fundamental Research, 2022.

Canonical source:
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667325821002168

DOI:
10.1016/j.fmre.2021.11.004

Rights:
Open access under a Creative Commons licence.

Finding:
The paper treats click-through rate as an upper-funnel response to the advertisement and conversion rate as a later response after the click, and finds that different text approaches may affect the two stages differently.

Safe candidate knowledge:
A creative that generates clicks is not necessarily the creative that generates conversions. Optimise and report CTR and post-click outcomes separately.

Limitations:
The study’s specific computational-ad setting and text categories must be retained.

Prohibited overclaim:
“High CTR means the ad is commercially effective.”

## Evidence record 11 — Crisis context changes how originality and appropriateness work

Source:
“Ad creativity in a negative context: How a thanking message frame enhances purchase intention in times of crisis”, Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services, 2022.

Canonical source:
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S096969892100391X

DOI:
10.1016/j.jretconser.2021.102825

Access:
Publisher metadata/abstract. Full reuse rights must be verified.

Design:
Analysis of 43,459 consumer responses to 49 retail and service advertisements in a crisis context.

Finding:
Originality and appropriateness behaved differently in a negatively valenced crisis context.

Safe candidate knowledge:
Creative evaluation must consider context. Originality, appropriateness and tone should be assessed separately, particularly during crises or sensitive events.

Limitations:
COVID-era crisis setting. Not a universal instruction to use gratitude framing.

Prohibited overclaim:
“Thank-you advertising increases purchase intent in every crisis.”

## Evidence record 12 — Social-ad response involves originality, liking, credibility and irritation

Source:
“Social Media Advertising Effectiveness”, International Journal of Technology and Human Interaction, 2022.

Canonical source:
https://www.sciencedirect.com/org/science/article/pii/S1548390822000144

DOI:
10.4018/IJTHI.300286

Access:
Open-access article.

Design:
Online survey of Facebook users.

Finding:
Originality, liking, credibility and irritation were associated with attitudes toward advertising, which in turn related to purchase intention and recommendation in the study.

Safe candidate knowledge:
Social-ad review should include credibility and irritation risk alongside originality and appeal.

Limitations:
Survey and intention measures do not prove observed purchasing behaviour or platform-causal impact.

Prohibited overclaim:
“Credible, original ads will always generate sales.”

## Cross-study synthesis for candidate Skill Cards

The following are suitable for review-state Skill Cards:

1. Repetition is not a universal performance rule.
2. Impressions, attention, recall, persuasion and conversion are separate outcomes.
3. Creative quality should balance novelty and relevance.
4. Creative effects may first appear in awareness, memory, liking or brand response.
5. B2B advertising should combine factual proof with creativity rather than assuming rational copy alone is optimal.
6. CTR and conversion rate describe different stages and can reward different creative choices.
7. Crisis and sensitive-context creative requires separate assessment of originality, appropriateness and tone.
8. Social-ad evaluation should include irritation and credibility risk.
9. B2B effects can be lagged, but lag is not an excuse for unsupported campaigns.
10. Source memory and brand recognition should be checked when campaigns use similar category conventions.

## Required review behaviour

Before any item is promoted:

- inspect the complete article where lawful access is available;
- record sample, geography, category, method and dependent variables;
- store DOI and licence;
- avoid converting association into causation;
- avoid converting intention into behaviour;
- avoid converting intermediate brand outcomes into revenue;
- set confidence and limitations;
- define which agents may retrieve the item.
