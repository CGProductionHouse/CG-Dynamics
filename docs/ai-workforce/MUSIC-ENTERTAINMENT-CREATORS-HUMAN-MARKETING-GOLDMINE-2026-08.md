## Core human truth

People do not become fans because a brand says an artist is “passionate”, “authentic” or “on a journey”.

People connect because:

- the work says something they feel;
- the artist has a recognisable point of view;
- the story feels specific and lived;
- the community makes them feel included;
- the event promises a real social experience;
- other people they trust are already talking about it;
- buying, saving, sharing or attending is easy;
- the campaign builds anticipation instead of dumping information.

The marketing job is to turn curiosity into belonging, and belonging into streams, saves, ticket sales, attendance, repeat participation and advocacy.

## Proven campaign and market examples

### Jazz in the Lights — culture-driven ticket funnel

Source: C&KE campaign case study for Arts Alive Joburg.

Reported results across 2023 and 2025:

- 325% increase in ticket sales;
- 70% of sales generated through digital touchpoints;
- 6.3x Meta ROAS;
- 170% year-on-year increase in digital engagement;
- 40% of 2025 buyers were first-time festival attendees.

What worked:

- phased launch rather than one announcement;
- artist and venue reveals;
- DJ and performer storytelling;
- behind-the-scenes access;
- nostalgia linked to contemporary city culture;
- warm, direct ticket CTAs;
- geographic campaign splits;
- final-week countdown pressure.

Transferable lesson:

A festival campaign should be built in chapters:

1. identity and date;
2. cultural promise;
3. line-up and performer stories;
4. social proof and community participation;
5. practical attendance information;
6. urgency and final ticket push;
7. post-event proof that strengthens the next edition.

### Bontgenoten — community trust before line-up

Source: Innercrowd case study.

Reported 2026 results:

- 14,000 tickets sold in under one hour;
- 13,100 sold directly through WhatsApp;
- 25% year-on-year improvement;
- case study reports 181% registration-to-conversion.

Human insight:

The audience trusted the festival concept before knowing the line-up. The organisers had built a community strong enough that identity, ritual and belonging carried the sale.

Transferable lesson:

- build a first-party fan list before launch day;
- make the sale path instant;
- reward insiders with early access;
- do not rely only on rented social reach;
- let recurring rituals become part of the product;
- measure list growth, registration, conversion and repeat purchase.

### Spotify Campaign Kit — release momentum in stages

Source: Spotify for Artists.

NERIAH campaign findings:

- nearly 500,000 new listeners reached in three months before the album;
- waterfall singles were used to create repeated moments;
- fans reached through the campaign streamed nearly four times more than other listeners.

Transferable lesson:

A release is not one post on release day. Strong campaigns sequence:

- audience warming;
- pre-save or follow action;
- teaser content;
- first single;
- second story angle;
- release-day conversion;
- post-release interpretation, live performance or fan reaction;
- deluxe, remix or tour follow-up where genuine.

### Rugby World Cup Sevens — entertainment as part of the event product

Source: Moonsport case study.

Reported outcome:

- more than 150,000 attendees;
- two of three days sold out;
- near-capacity stadium for most of the event;
- 29 acts, 4 resident DJs and 17 entertainment breaks.

Transferable lesson:

Entertainment should not be treated as decoration around the main event. It can become a reason to attend, stay longer, create content and return.

### Red Bull Kas’Lami — low-friction local access

Source: Mobitainment case study.

What worked:

- missed-call entry;
- reverse-billed USSD;
- mobile-first access;
- geo-targeted youth media;
- posters and community channels;
- competition mechanics accessible on entry-level phones.

Transferable lesson:

Never design an entertainment campaign only for expensive phones, high data use or card-heavy checkout when the real audience uses WhatsApp, USSD, cash, local media and community networks.

## Audience and fan behaviour

### Emerging artist audience

They need:

- one clear reason to care;
- a recognisable sound, story or personality;
- proof that other people are listening;
- repeated exposure without spam;
- easy ways to save, follow, share and attend;
- moments that make early support feel meaningful.

### Established artist audience

They respond to:

- new interpretation of a familiar identity;
- nostalgia without laziness;
- access, intimacy and insider status;
- live versions and behind-the-scenes context;
- fan recognition;
- limited experiences and meaningful merchandise;
- proof that the artist still understands the community.

### Festival and event audience

Their real questions are:

- Who is playing?
- Who else is going?
- What will the atmosphere feel like?
- Is it worth the money?
- Is the venue safe and practical?
- What time does everything happen?
- Will transport, parking, weather or queues ruin it?
- Can I attend with my people?
- What will I miss if I stay home?

## Human messaging angles

Strong angles:

- the song that says what the audience could not say;
- the place where a specific community gathers;
- the performer behind the public image;
- the one live moment that cannot be recreated later;
- the history or local culture behind the event;
- the fan who has been there since the beginning;
- the first-listen reaction;
- the rehearsal, mistake, recovery or breakthrough;
- the city, town or venue as a character;
- a real invitation from the artist, not a poster voice.

Avoid:

- “music is my passion”;
- “an unforgettable experience” with no proof;
- “where music meets magic”;
- “get ready for a night to remember”;
- “bringing people together through the power of music”;
- fake fan accounts;
- staged virality presented as organic;
- vague hype with no atmosphere, line-up, story or value.

## Commercial and short-form script structures

### Artist-release script

1. Start with a real feeling or situation.
2. Let the artist speak in natural language.
3. Show the lyric, sound or moment that carries the feeling.
4. Give one piece of context that deepens meaning.
5. End with a specific action: listen, save, follow, send it to someone, attend.

Example structure:

> “I wrote this after realising I was still waiting for an apology that was never coming. This is the line where I finally stopped waiting.”

Then move into the music.

### Event-hype script

1. Open on the atmosphere, not the logo.
2. Show the people the event is for.
3. Reveal what makes this edition specific.
4. Remove one practical objection.
5. End with date, place and ticket action.

### Fan-story script

1. Introduce the fan in their own words.
2. Show what the artist or event means in their life.
3. Include a specific memory.
4. Connect that memory to the upcoming release or event.
5. Invite others into the community.

### Behind-the-scenes script

1. Begin with a problem, decision or tension.
2. Show the work, rehearsal or adjustment.
3. Let people speak naturally.
4. Reveal the result.
5. Tie it to the public moment.

### Creator and influencer content

Use creators when they add:

- genuine audience overlap;
- local credibility;
- humour or storytelling skill;
- access to a real community;
- useful interpretation;
- event participation.

Do not hire creators only for follower count.

## Paid media strategy

### Release campaign

- warm existing listeners first;
- segment current followers, past engagers and new discovery audiences;
- use the strongest emotional or sonic moment, not generic cover art;
- send traffic to one clear action;
- retarget viewers who watched meaningfully;
- build repeated moments across the release cycle;
- compare saves, follows, completion, streams and repeat listening.

### Ticket campaign

- separate awareness from conversion;
- split by city or travel radius;
- use performer-specific ads where demand differs;
- retarget video viewers, site visitors and cart abandoners;
- update creative as sales stages change;
- use urgency only when true;
- optimise to verified ticket sales rather than link clicks.

### Tour campaign

- city-specific proof;
- local language and venue context;
- local media and creator support;
- city-level budgets tied to capacity and sales pace;
- dynamic urgency based on real availability;
- post-show content used to sell the next city.

## Rights-sensitive marketing rules

Include rights only where they affect marketing execution.

Before publishing or boosting content, verify:

- music recording rights;
- composition rights where relevant;
- performer release;
- venue permissions;
- event-filming notices;
- creator usage rights;
- paid-media usage rights;
- territory and duration;
- platform music-library restrictions;
- whether a trend sound may be used commercially.

Do not assume a song available on a platform is cleared for a brand advert.

## Measurement

### Artist campaign

- saves;
- follows;
- repeat listeners;
- playlist adds;
- completion or meaningful watch time;
- profile visits;
- pre-saves;
- streams by source;
- fan-list growth;
- ticket conversion where linked.

### Event campaign

- tickets sold;
- cost per ticket sale;
- ROAS;
- sales pace by phase;
- city and radius performance;
- cart abandonment;
- first-party list growth;
- repeat buyers;
- attendance versus tickets issued;
- check-in time and no-show rate;
- post-event waitlist growth.

Do not treat views, likes or reach as proof of ticket sales or fandom.

## Review-state Skill Cards

### ENTERTAINMENT-TICKET-CAMPAIGN

1. Define capacity, date, ticket tiers and break-even point.
2. Identify existing community and first-party audience.
3. Build the campaign in phased moments.
4. Prepare artist, atmosphere and practical-information creative.
5. Implement verified ticket tracking.
6. Split audiences by location, affinity and engagement state.
7. Monitor sales pace, not just traffic.
8. Change creative and urgency according to real ticket state.
9. Capture post-event proof for the next campaign.

### ARTIST-RELEASE-CAMPAIGN

1. Define the human story behind the release.
2. Identify the strongest lyric, sound or emotional hook.
3. Build pre-release, launch and post-release moments.
4. Prepare live, conversational and fan-facing content.
5. Choose one clear action per asset.
6. Track saves, follows, repeat listening and fan-list growth.
7. Extend momentum only with genuine new material.

### FAN-COMMUNITY-BUILD

1. Identify the existing fan rituals, language and gathering places.
2. Create a first-party channel such as email or WhatsApp.
3. Reward access and participation, not only spending.
4. Feature real fan stories with permission.
5. Build recurring moments the community can anticipate.
6. Measure repeat participation and referral.
