# Healthcare, Wellness and Medical Services — Human Marketing Goldmine

Last updated: 2026-08-06
Status: Research-backed, review-gated marketing source pack.
Scope: South African medical practices, specialists, allied health, dental, wellness clinics, preventative health, pharmacies and health-service brands.

## Purpose

Help CG Production House market healthcare and wellness clients with work that feels human, useful and trustworthy.

This is not a medical reference library. It is a marketing resource for:

- patient discovery;
- trust-building;
- ethical education;
- local search;
- appointment conversion;
- reputation;
- paid campaigns;
- community activations;
- useful content;
- compliant human storytelling.

Exclude bookkeeping, practice administration, coding, claims processing, payroll and unrelated clinical operations.

## Human truth of the category

People rarely search for a healthcare provider in a relaxed state. They are often worried, embarrassed, uncertain, in pain, short on time or afraid of cost.

The strongest marketing therefore does not begin with prestige. It begins with relief:

- Can you help with this problem?
- Will you listen to me?
- Will I understand what happens next?
- Can I trust your judgement?
- What will it cost?
- How soon can I be seen?
- Will I be treated with dignity?

The best healthcare marketing makes the next step feel safer and clearer without promising an outcome.

## Evidence and campaign lessons

### Dis-Chem Health — search visibility and performance media

Source: Microsoft Advertising case study.

Reported outcome:

- audience ads exceeded lead expectations;
- display activity contributed 38% of Microsoft Advertising sales;
- the campaign increased visibility while maintaining cost-effective acquisition.

Human lesson:

Healthcare advertising works when the offer and service are easy to understand at the exact moment a person is searching. Search intent should be matched to a clear next action, not buried under corporate healthcare language.

Transferable use:

- separate campaigns by service and patient need;
- use plain-language queries;
- route ads to the exact service page;
- measure booked consultations or verified sales, not clicks alone.

Source: https://about.ads.microsoft.com/en/resources/discover/case-studies/dis-chem-health-success-story

### Einstein Healthcare Network — people, not just patients

Source: Seer Interactive case study.

Reported outcome:

- 197% year-on-year increase in online appointment requests after restructuring paid search and improving targeting.

Human lesson:

The campaign worked from the premise that healthcare can feel cold and untrustworthy. The stronger creative and journey treated people as people, then connected that message to more relevant search campaigns.

Transferable use:

- organise paid search around actual conditions, services and locations;
- use practitioner and process proof;
- show what booking, arrival and consultation will feel like;
- remove vague hospital-style brand copy from high-intent landing pages.

Source: https://www.seerinteractive.com/work/case-studies/einstein-healthcare-ppc

### South African specialist-practice growth model

Source: Digitlab medical-practice case study.

Documented approach:

- stabilise lead generation before scaling;
- improve lead quality, not only volume;
- collect more meaningful information in the lead journey;
- reduce management waste and move budget into media.

Human lesson:

A calendar full of unsuitable enquiries is not success. Medical marketing needs to help the correct person recognise that the service fits their need before they submit.

Source: https://digitlab.co.za/case-study/marketing-strategy-for-medical-practice/

### Negative reviews carry disproportionate trust damage

Source: Journal of Consumer Marketing study on physician reviews.

Finding:

- positive reviews were considered more helpful;
- negative reviews had a stronger effect on trust intention.

Marketing implication:

One unresolved trust concern can outweigh a large amount of polished content. Reputation work must include service recovery, professional responses and private resolution paths, not simply requesting more five-star reviews.

Source: https://doi.org/10.1108/JCM-02-2023-5827

## South African patient-choice model

A person commonly moves through this sequence:

1. Notices a symptom, discomfort or life problem.
2. Searches the problem in ordinary language.
3. Looks for a nearby provider or recognised specialist.
4. Checks credentials, photos, reviews, cost clues and availability.
5. Looks for reassurance that the provider will listen and explain.
6. Compares the practical next step: call, WhatsApp, online booking or referral.
7. Delays when the website is unclear, frightening, clinical or difficult to use.

Marketing should support each stage without diagnosing the person through an ad.

## Audience groups

### The worried first-time patient

Needs:

- reassurance;
- plain explanation;
- clear booking steps;
- realistic expectations;
- privacy and dignity.

### The comparison patient

Needs:

- practitioner credentials;
- service distinctions;
- location and availability;
- cost or medical-aid clarity where verified;
- evidence of a professional patient experience.

### The referred patient

Needs:

- confirmation that the recommendation is credible;
- an easy way to verify the practitioner;
- directions, preparation and booking information.

### The returning or chronic-care patient

Needs:

- consistency;
- reminders;
- education;
- continuity;
- simple follow-up access.

### The wellness customer

Needs:

- realistic benefits;
- proof without miracle language;
- transparent pricing;
- a clear distinction between medical treatment, wellness support and lifestyle services.

## Human messaging principles

Use:

- the exact concern people express;
- the next useful step;
- a calm practitioner voice;
- process clarity;
- dignity;
- realistic support;
- verified proof.

Avoid:

- “your journey to wellness starts here”;
- “unlock the best version of yourself”;
- “world-class care” without evidence;
- “transform your life”;
- fear-based urgency;
- miracle, guaranteed or permanent-result claims;
- making people feel defective;
- pretending that every symptom has a simple answer.

## Commercial and short-form script structures

### Script 1 — The question people are embarrassed to ask

Hook:

“People often wait months before asking us this.”

Body:

- practitioner states the real question in respectful language;
- explains what is common and what deserves assessment;
- describes what happens during the first appointment;
- closes with a calm next step.

CTA:

“Book an assessment when you are ready to understand what is happening.”

### Script 2 — What your first visit actually looks like

Opening:

“Not knowing what happens at the appointment is often the worst part.”

Scenes:

- arrival;
- reception;
- private consultation;
- assessment equipment where appropriate;
- explanation and next-step discussion.

CTA:

“Here is how to prepare and what to bring.”

### Script 3 — The wrong assumption

Hook:

“A lot of people think this automatically means [common assumption]. It does not.”

Body:

- correct the misconception carefully;
- explain the range of possible causes without diagnosing;
- say when professional assessment is sensible.

### Script 4 — Cost and process clarity

Hook:

“Before you book, these are the three practical things most people want to know.”

Cover:

- consultation structure;
- verified price or price range where permitted;
- medical-aid or payment process;
- expected duration;
- follow-up pathway.

### Script 5 — Human practitioner introduction

Do not use a CV recital.

Use:

- why this field matters to the practitioner;
- the kind of patient concern they regularly help explain;
- how they want people to feel during the consultation;
- one personal detail that makes them approachable.

## Content goldmine

### Practitioner-led education

- “The question I wish patients asked sooner.”
- “What this symptom can and cannot tell you.”
- “When to monitor and when to make an appointment.”
- “What to bring to your first consultation.”
- “Three myths we hear every week.”
- “What happens after the test?”
- “What a normal follow-up process looks like.”

All clinical content must be reviewed and attributed to the qualified practitioner.

### Trust and process content

- reception and arrival walkthrough;
- parking and access;
- consultation-room introduction;
- equipment explanation;
- meet the practitioner and support team;
- what privacy looks like in practice;
- how results and follow-ups are communicated;
- how urgent concerns are handled.

### Human stories without exploitation

Use:

- practitioner motivation;
- anonymised patterns only where ethically approved;
- staff care moments;
- community health initiatives;
- patient education days;
- recovery support explained through process rather than dramatic before/after promises.

Do not use a patient’s vulnerability as emotional content bait.

### Photography direction

Capture:

- eye contact;
- listening;
- explanation;
- calm, clean spaces;
- accessible arrival;
- real equipment in use;
- staff interaction;
- practitioner personality.

Avoid:

- fake clipboard poses;
- folded-arms doctor portraits only;
- random stethoscope close-ups;
- stock-model pain faces;
- sterile blue overlays;
- invasive imagery used as shock content.

## Paid advertising

### Google Search

Separate by:

- condition or problem intent;
- service;
- practitioner type;
- location;
- urgent versus planned need;
- branded versus non-branded search.

Landing pages must show:

- who the service is for;
- what happens;
- practitioner credentials;
- location;
- booking method;
- verified fees or payment information where appropriate;
- what the service cannot promise.

### Meta

Best suited to:

- awareness and education;
- wellness offers where compliant;
- practitioner familiarity;
- events and screenings;
- remarketing to non-sensitive website audiences where lawful;
- community health initiatives.

Avoid ad copy that asserts or implies a private health condition about the viewer.

### YouTube

Useful formats:

- practitioner explainers;
- procedure or consultation walkthroughs;
- patient-preparation videos;
- myth correction;
- longer human stories with explicit consent and professional review.

## SEO and local search themes

### Provider intent

- [specialist type] near me;
- [specialist type] Bloemfontein;
- private clinic [location];
- dentist open Saturday [location];
- physiotherapist for [problem] [location];
- psychologist taking new patients [location].

### Problem intent

Build clinically reviewed pages around the phrases people actually use, then distinguish education from diagnosis.

Examples:

- when should I see a [specialist];
- why does [problem] keep happening;
- what happens during [assessment];
- how long does [appointment/procedure] take;
- what should I bring to [consultation].

### Trust intent

- practitioner name;
- practitioner qualifications;
- clinic reviews;
- medical-aid acceptance;
- consultation fee;
- directions and parking;
- languages spoken;
- accessibility.

## Website conversion standard

Every healthcare site should make these answers easy to find:

- What do you help with?
- Who will I see?
- Where are you?
- How do I book?
- What happens next?
- How much does it cost, or how is cost confirmed?
- Do I need a referral?
- What should I bring?
- What happens in an emergency?

Do not hide basic information behind a contact form.

## Reviews and reputation

Ethical review system:

1. Ask consistently after an appropriate service moment.
2. Never pressure a vulnerable patient.
3. Do not offer rewards for positive reviews.
4. Respond without confirming that the reviewer is a patient.
5. Move complaints into a private service-recovery process.
6. Track recurring concerns by theme.
7. Fix the patient experience before trying to bury criticism.

## Promotions, activations and giveaways

Useful ideas:

- community screening or education day with qualified oversight;
- “ask the practitioner” evening;
- workplace wellness session;
- school or sports-club education partnership;
- first-aid or prevention workshop;
- local pharmacy or allied-health referral event;
- practical downloadable preparation checklist;
- wellness challenge focused on habits rather than body shame;
- free educational talk rather than a questionable treatment giveaway.

Safer giveaway examples:

- ergonomic assessment;
- health-literacy book bundle;
- approved wellness class pass;
- first-aid kit;
- consultation voucher only where professionally and ethically permitted;
- community donation linked to participation.

## Books and human sources

Useful human-led sources for the marketing team:

- *The Language of Kindness* — Christie Watson: emotional reality of care.
- *Complications* — Atul Gawande: uncertainty, judgement and the humanity of medicine.
- *Being Mortal* — Atul Gawande: dignity and difficult conversations.
- *The Checklist Manifesto* — Atul Gawande: making complex processes understandable.
- *Made to Stick* — Chip Heath and Dan Heath: memorable explanation without jargon.
- *The Mom Test* — Rob Fitzpatrick: useful interview discipline for understanding real needs.

These are not clinical instruction sources. They help writers and strategists understand communication, trust, decision-making and humane service.

## Marketing-specific compliance gate

Before publishing or promoting:

- verify the practitioner and credentials;
- verify every treatment, outcome and comparative claim;
- obtain documented consent for identifiable patient material;
- protect personal health information;
- avoid guarantees;
- avoid manufactured scarcity around health;
- distinguish education from diagnosis;
- review current HPCSA, pharmacy, product and platform requirements relevant to the exact client;
- do not target people using unlawfully obtained health data;
- do not use testimonials or before/after content without exact current professional review.

## Measurement

Track:

- qualified appointment requests;
- booked appointments;
- attendance rate;
- cost per attended appointment;
- suitable versus unsuitable enquiry rate;
- calls and WhatsApp conversations;
- local-search actions;
- review recency and themes;
- practitioner-page conversion;
- referral-source quality;
- repeat or follow-up engagement where appropriate.

Do not present impressions, clicks or raw leads as patient acquisition success.

## Review-state Skill Cards

### HEALTHCARE-HUMAN-CONTENT-BRIEF

1. Start with one real patient question.
2. Confirm the qualified reviewer.
3. Define what may and may not be claimed.
4. Explain the process in plain language.
5. Add one practical next step.
6. Remove fear bait, prestige filler and generic wellness language.
7. Record approval and review date.

### HEALTHCARE-LOCAL-BOOKING-AUDIT

1. Search the service and location as a patient would.
2. Check Google Business Profile accuracy.
3. Review practitioner and service pages.
4. Test mobile booking, calling and WhatsApp.
5. Verify fees, referral needs, hours, parking and directions.
6. Review recent reputation themes.
7. record conversion friction and priority fixes.

### HEALTHCARE-PAID-LEAD-QUALITY-REVIEW

1. Separate leads by service and search intent.
2. Check suitability and appointment outcome.
3. Identify misleading or broad keywords.
4. Review landing-page promise and qualification.
5. exclude sensitive, irrelevant and low-intent traffic.
6. optimise toward attended appointments, not form volume.

## Exclusions

Do not add:

- generic health articles with no marketing application;
- copied symptom encyclopaedias;
- medical advice generated without practitioner review;
- bookkeeping or medical-aid administration;
- sensational patient stories;
- miracle wellness claims;
- “feel better, live better” AI filler;
- unaudited influencer health claims;
- performance claims without source and context.
