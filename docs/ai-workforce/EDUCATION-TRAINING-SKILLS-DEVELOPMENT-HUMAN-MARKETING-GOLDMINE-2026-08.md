# Education, Training and Skills Development Human Marketing Goldmine

Last updated: 2026-08-06
Status: Researched marketing pack; all derived knowledge and skill cards remain review-gated.
Jurisdiction focus: South Africa, with transferable international evidence clearly labelled.
Scope: Schools, colleges, universities, TVET, online learning, short courses, professional training, learnerships and skills development.

## Purpose

This pack exists to help CG Production House market education and training providers in a way that is useful, human, ethical and commercially effective.

It focuses on:

- qualified applications and enrolments;
- parent, learner and employer trust;
- course choice and career relevance;
- student experience and support;
- open days, webinars, campus visits and demonstrations;
- local search and programme discovery;
- student and alumni stories;
- lead quality, follow-up and registration conversion;
- retention and re-enrolment where marketing has a legitimate role;
- honest reporting from inquiry through attended learner.

It excludes generic academic administration, payroll, tax and operational material unless it directly affects a marketing promise or learner experience.

## Human decision truths

Education is a high-anxiety, high-consequence purchase. The buyer may be the learner, parent, employer, funder or some combination of them.

Common questions are:

- Is this institution legitimate and recognised?
- Will this qualification or course help me get work, progress or change direction?
- Can I afford it, and what is included?
- Will I cope academically and practically?
- Is the location, timetable, data requirement or transport realistic?
- What happens when I struggle?
- Are the facilities, lecturers and student support as good as the advertising suggests?
- What do graduates actually do afterwards?
- Is online learning genuinely supported or am I being sold recorded videos and left alone?

A South African University of Pretoria study specifically investigated the importance of institutional choice factors and information sources used by first-year students. A University of Johannesburg study on MOOC enrolment reinforces that motivation depends on the learner and technology environment, not only the course topic.

### Marketing implication

Do not market only prestige or vague transformation. Show:

- the actual programme;
- who it suits;
- what the learning experience looks like;
- entry requirements;
- cost and payment truth;
- timetable and delivery mode;
- support available;
- realistic progression routes;
- verified outcomes and limitations.

## South African campaign and brand evidence

### 1. UNISA 2024 applications and enrolments campaign

Source: Basefive case study.

Reported objective:

- drive applications and enrolments for the 2024 academic year;
- reach prospective students nationally;
- position UNISA as accessible, flexible and future-ready.

Reusable lesson:

A large education brand needs a full recruitment system, not disconnected awareness posts. Accessibility, flexibility and future relevance are stronger when connected to a clear application path and dates.

Limitation:

The public case-study page confirms the brief and strategy direction but does not expose enough audited conversion data to support a hard ROI claim.

### 2. University of Stellenbosch Business School digital lead generation

Source: Huble case study.

Agency-reported result:

- more than 10,000 leads in six months.

Reusable lesson:

Reputation alone does not remove the need for a structured digital funnel. High-interest programmes still need audience segmentation, useful landing pages, lead capture, CRM follow-up and programme-specific messaging.

Limitation:

Lead volume is not enrolment volume. The public claim should not be converted into registrations or revenue without first-party admissions data.

### 3. Saving Grace Education Group national enrolment campaign

Sources: Saving Grace Education Group and Bizcommunity.

Documented campaign elements:

- nationwide billboard rollout in Gauteng for 2026 enrolments;
- CAPS, Cambridge and Special Needs pathways;
- early-registration pricing incentive;
- online-school positioning around flexibility and access.

Reusable lesson:

Online education can benefit from physical media because parents need legitimacy and familiarity, not only digital targeting. A billboard can create confidence while search, website and consultation channels close the decision.

Limitation:

Public material documents the campaign and offer but does not provide independently verified enrolment lift.

### 4. UST College low-data enrolment platform

Source: Codit case study.

Documented problem:

- extend training access to areas where programmes are difficult to reach;
- account for data cost, device limitations and constrained connectivity.

Reusable lesson:

In South African education marketing, the enrolment experience itself is part of the campaign. A beautiful site that cannot work on a basic phone or affordable connection undermines the access promise.

### 5. Stock Market College paid-media case

Source: Future Famous case study.

Agency-reported result:

- approximately 3.5x ROAS.

Reusable lesson:

High-intent education categories can use paid search and retargeting effectively when the programme, audience and conversion event are tightly defined.

Risk:

Trading and financial education must never imply guaranteed income, trading success or investment outcomes. Any result belongs to the marketing campaign, not learner financial performance.

### 6. Reddam House enrolment-lead campaign

Source: G Studio case study.

Documented objective:

- elevate brand perception;
- strengthen global-leader positioning;
- increase qualified enrolment leads through integrated channels.

Reusable lesson:

Private-school marketing must sell an educational environment and fit, not only facilities. Brand, parent trust, learning culture, campus experience and admissions follow-up must match.

Limitation:

The public case page describes strategy and goals but does not expose enough audited outcomes for a hard performance claim.

## International evidence worth adapting

### Gettysburg College recruitment system

Source: Carnegie case study.

Reported contribution:

- CollegeXpress leads represented 10% of admitted students and 15% of Fall 2023 net deposits.

Reusable lesson:

Measure sources through the recruitment funnel. A channel with fewer inquiries may contribute disproportionately to admitted or deposited students.

### Landmark College enrolment engine

Source: Belfort Group case study.

Documented approach:

- multi-year enrolment campaign;
- lead nurturing;
- call-centre qualification;
- CRM implementation;
- admissions-team training.

Reusable lesson:

Marketing cannot fix an admissions process that does not respond, qualify, nurture and close. The handoff between campaign and admissions is part of marketing performance.

## Buyer segments

### School parent

Needs:

- safety;
- academic fit;
- support;
- values and culture;
- transport and convenience;
- communication quality;
- transparent fees;
- evidence of learner development.

### School learner

Needs:

- belonging;
- activities;
- friendships;
- subject and career relevance;
- a realistic sense of daily life.

### Matric learner choosing tertiary study

Needs:

- programme fit;
- accreditation and legitimacy;
- entry requirements;
- funding and cost;
- campus life;
- career direction;
- application help;
- reassurance about uncertainty.

### Working adult

Needs:

- timetable flexibility;
- employer relevance;
- practical value;
- credible certification;
- manageable workload;
- online support;
- clear cost and duration.

### Employer or funder

Needs:

- skills relevance;
- provider credibility;
- completion evidence;
- reporting;
- practical transfer to work;
- group delivery and support.

### TVET or vocational learner

Needs:

- practical experience;
- workshop and equipment proof;
- workplace pathway;
- realistic job information;
- transport, residence and location clarity;
- registration and funding guidance.

## Customer-language research

Before writing campaigns, collect language from:

- enquiry calls and WhatsApp;
- admissions interviews;
- open-day questions;
- Google reviews;
- student support tickets;
- course feedback;
- dropout or non-registration reasons;
- career-counselling sessions;
- employer feedback;
- public student discussions, used cautiously and never treated as representative by default.

Common language patterns include:

- “Will this actually help me get a job?”
- “Is the qualification recognised?”
- “Can I study while working?”
- “What happens if I fall behind?”
- “What am I paying for?”
- “Is there practical training?”
- “Do you help with applications or funding?”
- “What do former students do now?”
- “Will my child be known or disappear in the system?”

## Positioning directions

Useful positioning is specific:

- practical training for a defined role;
- flexible study for working adults;
- small classes and visible support;
- a clear pathway from subject choice to career;
- recognised training with transparent requirements;
- parent communication and learner care;
- accessible online study designed for low-data environments;
- industry-linked teaching with named workplace exposure;
- a school culture visible through real people and daily life.

Avoid:

- “unlock your potential”;
- “shape your future”;
- “future-ready learning” without proof;
- “world-class education” without evidence;
- “your journey starts here”;
- “empowering tomorrow’s leaders”;
- guaranteed-employment language;
- unsupported salary or pass-rate claims.

## Human commercial structures

### 1. The real question

Hook: A learner or parent asks the question they are actually worried about.

Example structure:

- “I work full time. Can I realistically finish this course?”
- show timetable and support;
- introduce one current learner;
- show how they study;
- explain who the course is and is not for;
- clear application action.

### 2. A day in the programme

- arrival or login;
- first lesson;
- practical activity;
- lecturer interaction;
- break and peer life;
- support resource;
- honest closing reflection.

### 3. What happens after this course?

- start with a defined programme;
- show the skills taught;
- explain realistic pathways;
- feature alumni or employer evidence where permission exists;
- state limits clearly;
- invite a career or admissions consultation.

### 4. Parent-to-parent reassurance

- real parent concern;
- what they checked;
- what changed their mind;
- one specific experience;
- invitation to visit rather than a hard sell.

### 5. Course comparison

- compare delivery mode, duration, entry requirements and practical content;
- explain who each option suits;
- never manipulate by hiding disadvantages.

### 6. Lecturer explains one difficult concept

- one useful concept;
- plain-language explanation;
- quick practical demonstration;
- connection to the full programme.

### 7. From application confusion to next step

- show the exact point of confusion;
- give a simple checklist;
- explain deadlines and documents;
- route to a real support person.

## Content system

### Proof content

- student work;
- practical demonstrations;
- workshop or classroom walkthroughs;
- lecturer expertise;
- accredited programme information;
- student-support process;
- verified alumni pathways;
- employer partnerships;
- open-day footage;
- graduation and completion stories.

### Decision-help content

- how to choose between programmes;
- entry-requirement explainers;
- course duration and workload;
- online versus contact study;
- funding and payment explanations;
- subject-choice guidance;
- what to bring to an open day;
- application-document checklist;
- what happens after applying.

### Human culture content

- student clubs;
- classroom moments;
- teacher personalities;
- residence or commute reality;
- school traditions;
- peer collaboration;
- staff who support learners behind the scenes.

### Outcome content

Use only verified outcomes:

- applications completed;
- enrolments;
- attendance;
- completion;
- assessment or pass results with method and period;
- employment or progression with sample and method;
- employer feedback;
- learner portfolios.

Do not turn one successful graduate into a universal promise.

## Paid-media strategy

### Google Search

High-intent groups:

- course name + city;
- school or college applications;
- programme entry requirements;
- accredited course;
- part-time or online course;
- learnership or skills programme;
- open day;
- application closing date;
- fees and payment options;
- TVET or vocational programme by trade;
- professional certification by role.

Landing pages should match the exact programme, location and intake.

### Meta

Best uses:

- parent and learner storytelling;
- open days;
- programme discovery;
- student-life video;
- lead nurturing;
- retargeting;
- reminders for deadlines and events.

Lead forms must collect enough information to qualify without becoming unusably long.

### YouTube

Best uses:

- campus and programme walkthroughs;
- practical demonstrations;
- student stories;
- parent information;
- application help;
- longer career explainers.

### TikTok and Reels

Best uses:

- day-in-the-life;
- lecturer personality;
- practical skills;
- student questions;
- quick application guidance;
- campus culture.

Do not force youth slang through staff who do not naturally speak that way.

### LinkedIn

Best uses:

- executive education;
- employer-funded training;
- professional programmes;
- alumni and faculty authority;
- B2B skills partnerships;
- employer outcomes.

Minimum programme page:

- exact programme name;
- provider and campus;
- accreditation or recognition truth;
- entry requirements;
- duration;
- delivery mode;
- timetable where available;
- curriculum summary;
- practical component;
- fees and material costs where permitted;
- funding or payment guidance;
- application dates;
- documents required;
- support available;
- realistic progression routes;
- contact and application action;
- FAQs based on actual enquiries.

Local SEO:

- accurate Google Business Profile;
- correct institution category;
- campus-specific details;
- current opening hours;
- current photos;
- review responses;
- no fake campus or location pages.

## Open days and activations

Strong activations:

- hands-on workshop sessions;
- mini lectures;
- parent Q&A rooms;
- career-pathway consultations;
- student-led campus tours;
- portfolio reviews;
- course tasters;
- application clinics;
- employer demonstration days;
- school subject-choice evenings;
- online live classrooms;
- alumni panels with specific, honest career stories.

Every activation should generate:

- questions;
- reusable video;
- testimonials with permission;
- application or consultation opportunities;
- follow-up segments based on interest.

## Giveaways

Useful:

- course bursary or partial-fee support with transparent rules;
- study kit;
- textbook or software support;
- data support for online learners;
- transport support for an open day;
- career-assessment session;
- practical tool kit linked to a vocational programme;
- school or classroom resource sponsorship.

Weak:

- generic electronics with no connection to the programme;
- competitions that collect thousands of irrelevant leads;
- “tag ten friends” mechanics that create vanity reach but no intent.

## Admissions funnel

Track separately:

1. impression or reach;
2. programme-page visit;
3. inquiry;
4. qualified inquiry;
5. counselling or admissions contact;
6. application started;
7. application completed;
8. accepted or eligible;
9. deposit or registration payment;
10. registered learner;
11. attended learner;
12. retained beyond the relevant early-risk period.

A lead is not an application. An application is not an enrolment. An enrolment is not attendance.

## Follow-up standards

- respond while the learner still remembers the campaign;
- identify programme, intake and location immediately;
- answer the learner’s actual question before pushing an application;
- record objections and non-conversion reasons;
- send deadline reminders without harassment;
- suppress invalid, unsuitable or opted-out leads;
- route academic, funding and disability-support questions to competent staff;
- do not pressure vulnerable learners into unsuitable debt or courses.

## Measurement

Report:

- cost per inquiry;
- cost per qualified inquiry;
- application-start rate;
- application-completion rate;
- acceptance or eligibility rate;
- registration rate;
- cost per registered learner;
- show-up or attendance rate;
- channel contribution to actual registrations;
- programme and intake performance;
- lead response time;
- reason for non-registration;
- source limitations and offline gaps.

For long decision cycles, use cohort reporting rather than declaring campaigns unsuccessful before applications close.

## Claim-risk register

High risk:

- guaranteed employment;
- guaranteed salary;
- guaranteed university admission;
- “fully accredited” without current evidence;
- misleading qualification level;
- false government or industry endorsement;
- unsupported pass rates;
- unsupported graduate employment rates;
- hidden fees;
- fake scarcity around spaces;
- presenting a short course as a formal qualification;
- financial-trading education that implies predictable returns.

Required control:

Every material claim must link to a current source, period, method and owner.

## Photography and video direction

Show:

- real learners;
- real lecturers;
- real work;
- real spaces;
- tools, equipment and teaching moments;
- support interactions;
- age-appropriate consent and safeguarding;
- diversity that reflects the actual institution.

Avoid:

- stock graduates throwing caps for every course;
- empty classrooms staged as “vibrant learning”;
- fake laboratory or workshop use;
- children used as generic emotional props;
- over-polished content that hides the real environment.

## Evidence rules

- Agency-reported results remain labelled as agency-reported.
- Lead counts are not enrolments.
- Applications are not registrations.
- Graduate anecdotes are not employment-rate evidence.
- Public student discussions can reveal questions but cannot establish market prevalence.
- Accreditation and programme claims require current institution or regulator evidence.
- Full copyrighted source text is not ingested; retain metadata, short summary and canonical link.
