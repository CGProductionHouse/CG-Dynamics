# 1. Building materials and home improvement

## 1.1 Market reality

This category includes retailers, manufacturers, distributors, specialist suppliers, hardware stores, paint businesses, plumbing suppliers, timber merchants, flooring, roofing, insulation, tools, sanitaryware, electrical suppliers and renovation-focused service businesses.

The buying journey is often split across several people:

- homeowner or property owner;
- contractor or installer;
- architect, designer or specifier;
- quantity surveyor or procurement person;
- developer or project manager;
- store buyer or trade customer;
- do-it-yourself consumer.

Marketing must distinguish retail convenience from specification-led project sales. A homeowner may be choosing by look, confidence and budget. A contractor may care more about stock, lead time, compatibility, technical support and margin. A professional specifier may require standards, documentation and proven performance.

## 1.2 Buyer pains

- Uncertainty about product suitability
- Wrong quantities and costly wastage
- Products being out of stock at the wrong time
- Delayed deliveries affecting contractors and project programmes
- Poor colour, finish or material selection
- Confusion between product grades and specifications
- Unclear installation requirements
- Hidden accessories or additional materials needed
- Fear of premature failure
- Lack of trustworthy technical advice
- Difficulty comparing whole-project cost rather than unit price
- Poor coordination between homeowner, contractor and supplier
- Inconsistent quality between batches or suppliers
- Returns and warranty disputes

## 1.3 Buying triggers

- New build or renovation start
- Contractor appointment
- Approved plans or finalised specification
- Seasonal maintenance
- Water damage, roof failure, cracking or wear
- Property sale preparation
- Store promotion or trade day
- Product failure or replacement
- New colour scheme or design change
- Project-budget approval
- Stock availability becoming urgent
- Need to comply with a technical or municipal requirement

## 1.4 Audience roles

- Homeowner planning a once-off project
- DIY buyer needing simple guidance
- Contractor needing repeat supply
- Architect or designer specifying a product
- Quantity surveyor comparing cost and availability
- Developer managing multiple units
- Trade reseller
- Facilities or maintenance manager
- Property investor improving resale or rental value

## 1.5 Campaign and ad angles

### Confidence before purchase

- Explain how to choose the right product for the use case.
- Show differences between grades, finishes, sizes and systems.
- Avoid oversimplified claims such as “best” or “maintenance-free” without evidence.

### Project planning

- Help buyers calculate quantity, accessories, lead time and delivery.
- Use checklists and planning tools that reduce waste and rework.

### Proof through use

- Show real installations, product application and completed projects.
- Include surface preparation, installation method and limitations where relevant.

### Trade reliability

- Emphasise stock visibility, delivery, account service, technical help and repeat supply where true.
- Separate trade offers from retail promotions.

### Design inspiration linked to action

- Use visual inspiration, but connect it to product names, finish, quantity, preparation and availability.
- Do not leave the buyer with attractive content but no practical next step.

## 1.6 Hook directions

- “Before you buy, check whether it is made for this surface.”
- “The hidden items most renovation budgets forget.”
- “How much do you actually need?”
- “Cheap per unit can become expensive per project.”
- “Three mistakes that cause this finish to fail early.”
- “What your contractor needs from the supplier before work starts.”
- “This is the difference between looking good today and lasting.”
- “What to ask before accepting a substitute product.”

## 1.7 Proof assets

- Product data sheets
- Installation guidance
- Standards or certification references where applicable
- Batch, warranty and return information
- Real project photography
- Before-and-after evidence
- Contractor or professional testimony with permission
- Stock and delivery process
- Colour, finish or material samples
- Quantity calculator or project worksheet
- Staff technical expertise
- Demonstration videos
- Long-term maintenance instructions

## 1.8 Offers and lead magnets

- Project quantity estimate
- Trade account application
- Product compatibility consultation
- Colour or finish consultation
- Sample request
- Contractor supply quote
- Delivery planning call
- Renovation checklist
- Material comparison guide
- Product-system bundle
- Seasonal maintenance guide
- Technical demonstration day

## 1.9 Content formats

- Side-by-side product comparison
- Installation sequence
- Common failure diagnosis
- Staff technical Q&A
- Contractor case study
- Before-and-after project story
- Product-system explainer
- Quantity-calculation walkthrough
- “What is included” bundle breakdown
- Stock and delivery update
- Design inspiration with exact product mapping
- Myth versus fact

## 1.10 Landing-page structure

1. Name the project type or problem.
2. Identify the buyer and use case.
3. Explain suitable product options.
4. Show proof, specifications and limitations.
5. Add quantity, preparation, delivery and installation information.
6. Answer stock, return, warranty and lead-time questions.
7. Use one clear action: quote, sample, consultation, trade account or order enquiry.

## 1.11 Objection handling

- “It is too expensive” → compare lifecycle, waste, failure risk and total installed cost.
- “The cheaper product looks the same” → explain grade, suitability, warranty and use-case difference.
- “I do not know how much I need” → provide quantity support.
- “My contractor will decide” → give contractor-ready documentation and shared consultation.
- “I need it immediately” → show honest stock and delivery options.
- “I am worried about the colour or finish” → offer samples and real-project references.

## 1.12 Retargeting stages

- Inspiration and problem awareness
- Product education
- Comparison and proof
- Quantity or quote assistance
- Stock and delivery reassurance
- Abandoned enquiry follow-up
- Post-purchase installation guidance
- Maintenance and repeat supply

## 1.13 Meaningful metrics

- Qualified quote requests
- Sample requests
- Trade account applications
- Store visits linked to campaign activity
- Quote-to-sale conversion
- Average order value
- Repeat trade purchases
- Product-category revenue contribution
- Delivery enquiries converted
- Cost per qualified project lead
- Lead quality by homeowner, contractor or professional
- Landing-page completion actions

Do not present reach, clicks or video views as project sales.

# 2. Hospitality, restaurants, bars and events

## 2.1 Market reality

Hospitality purchases are occasion-driven. Customers choose based on the combination of food, drink, atmosphere, convenience, social proof, event relevance, price, availability and expected experience.

The same venue may serve several distinct audiences:

- everyday local diners;
- families;
- couples;
- business lunches;
- sports viewers;
- nightlife audiences;
- private-event organisers;
- tourists;
- regulars;
- corporate groups.

Marketing should not treat all audiences as one generic “people looking for a good time” group.

## 2.2 Buyer pains

- Not knowing what the venue feels like before arriving
- Unclear menu, pricing or availability
- Difficulty booking
- Slow responses to enquiries
- Fear that the venue will not suit the occasion
- Uncertainty about parking, safety, accessibility or family suitability
- Poor consistency between advertised and real experience
- Limited proof for private events
- Confusion about specials and conditions
- Concern about service quality during busy periods
- Lack of dietary or practical information
- Event information being scattered across social posts
- Customers forgetting to return after one visit

## 2.3 Buying triggers

- Birthday, anniversary or celebration
- Weekend planning
- Live sport
- Public holiday
- Payday or month-end
- Seasonal menu or promotion
- Live music or entertainment
- Business meeting or staff function
- Tourism or travel
- Family outing
- Date night
- Group booking
- Limited-time event
- Recommendation from friends or social proof

## 2.4 Audience roles

- Person choosing for a group
- Family decision-maker
- Couple planning an occasion
- Corporate organiser
- Sports fan
- Tourist or visitor
- Regular local customer
- Event planner
- Influencer or creator partner
- Walk-in customer comparing nearby venues

## 2.5 Campaign and ad angles

### Occasion-led marketing

- Sell the reason to visit, not only the product.
- Connect menu, atmosphere and service to the customer’s occasion.

### Experience proof

- Show real tables, real food, real people and real service.
- Avoid presenting staged content that creates an experience the venue cannot deliver.

### Signature product or ritual

- Build recognition around a specific dish, drink, service moment, event format or venue tradition.

### Local belonging

- Use local language, humour and community references authentically.
- Avoid forcing generic trend content that weakens the venue’s identity.

### Event clarity

- Date, time, booking method, cost, inclusions, age restrictions and terms must be clear.
- Do not create false scarcity or hide important conditions.

### Repeat-visit system

- Give customers a reason to return beyond the first special.
- Use series, recurring events, menu discovery and community participation.

## 2.6 Hook directions

- “This is what your Friday night could look like.”
- “The table is the main event.”
- “What to order when the whole group wants something different.”
- “The reason regulars keep coming back.”
- “Before the first whistle, this is where the atmosphere starts.”
- “A private event without the planning chaos.”
- “The dish people tell their friends about.”
- “Not just a special — this is the occasion.”

## 2.7 Proof assets

- Real food and drink photography
- Service-in-action footage
- Venue walkthrough
- Booking process
- Event setup examples
- Customer testimonials with permission
- Group-table or private-event examples
- Menu and pricing clarity
- Staff personalities
- Real peak-time atmosphere
- Accessibility and practical information
- Consistent visual identity
- Event outcomes such as attendance or repeat booking, where available

## 2.8 Offers and lead magnets

- Table booking
- Private-event enquiry
- Group menu
- Birthday package
- Sports-viewing booking
- Recurring weekly special
- Set menu
- Tasting event
- Venue walkthrough
- Event-planning checklist
- Corporate function enquiry
- Loyalty or return-visit offer

Alcohol-related promotions must be reviewed for applicable law, platform restrictions, age targeting and responsible-marketing requirements before use.

## 2.9 Content formats

- Dish story
- Staff recommendation
- Table reaction
- Behind-the-scenes preparation
- Venue atmosphere reel
- Event announcement
- Event recap
- Recurring weekly series
- Customer occasion story
- Menu explainer
- “What to expect” booking walkthrough
- Chef, bartender or host personality content
- Sports-day build-up
- Before-and-after event setup

## 2.10 Landing-page structure

1. Name the occasion or experience.
2. Show real venue atmosphere.
3. Present menu, package or event information clearly.
4. Include practical details: date, time, location, parking, booking and terms.
5. Show social proof and previous events.
6. Answer group size, deposit, dietary and cancellation questions.
7. Use one action: book, enquire or WhatsApp.

## 2.11 Objection handling

- “Will everyone find something to eat?” → show range and group options.
- “Will it be too noisy or too quiet?” → show the real atmosphere for that occasion.
- “Can you handle our group?” → show capacity, setup and booking process.
- “Is the special really worth it?” → explain exact inclusions and conditions.
- “I am worried about service during events” → show planning, staffing and booking controls.
- “We do not know where to go” → make location, parking and arrival easy.

## 2.12 Retargeting stages

- Discovery and atmosphere
- Menu or event consideration
- Social proof
- Booking reminder
- Abandoned enquiry follow-up
- Pre-event confirmation
- Post-visit return offer
- Recurring event reminder
- Private-event nurturing

## 2.13 Meaningful metrics

- Bookings
- Qualified private-event enquiries
- Booking completion rate
- Cost per booking
- Table occupancy contribution
- Event attendance
- Repeat-visit rate
- Group booking value
- Special redemption
- Menu-page or booking-page actions
- Enquiry response time
- No-show rate
- Customer database growth with consent

Do not treat video views as restaurant revenue.

# 3. Property, architecture and construction services

## 3.1 Market reality

This category includes architects, designers, engineers, builders, project managers, quantity surveyors, property developers, estate agents, renovation specialists and related professional services.

The sales cycle is often long and high-trust. Prospective clients may be planning one of the largest purchases or investments of their lives. They are evaluating competence, design fit, process, approvals, budget control, communication and delivery risk.

Marketing must reduce uncertainty without promising approvals, costs, timelines or outcomes that depend on third parties or unknown project conditions.

## 3.2 Buyer pains

- Not knowing where to start
- Fear of cost overruns
- Unclear professional fees
- Poor understanding of approvals and stages
- Difficulty comparing service providers
- Concern about being ignored after appointment
- Bad previous contractor or consultant experience
- Uncertainty about project feasibility
- Fear that design will not match lifestyle or business needs
- Confusion about who is responsible for what
- Delays caused by incomplete information
- Lack of confidence in drawings or technical decisions
- Difficulty understanding the value of professional services
- Concern about resale, long-term use or maintenance

## 3.3 Buying triggers

- Land purchase
- Property purchase
- Family growth or lifestyle change
- Business expansion
- Renovation need
- New development opportunity
- Damage or building failure
- Municipal submission requirement
- Funding or investment approval
- Need for additional income-producing space
- Change of use
- Property sale preparation
- Compliance or regularisation issue

## 3.4 Audience roles

- Homeowner
- Couple or family planning a home
- Property investor
- Developer
- Business owner
- Body corporate or facilities manager
- Landowner
- Estate agent or referring professional
- Contractor seeking professional coordination
- Public or institutional client

## 3.5 Campaign and ad angles

### Process clarity

- Explain stages from brief to concept, approvals, technical documentation and construction involvement.
- Make the invisible professional work understandable.

### Decision confidence

- Show how options are tested against site, budget, function, climate and long-term use.
- Avoid reducing architecture to attractive renders only.

### Real project evidence

- Use completed work, construction progress, drawings and post-occupancy lessons.
- Separate concept imagery from built reality.

### Risk reduction

- Explain coordination, documentation, approvals, procurement and site involvement.
- Do not promise that professional involvement removes all project risk.

### Local and contextual expertise

- Show understanding of local climate, materials, regulations, suppliers and building practice where genuinely applicable.

### Commercial value

- For business or development clients, connect design decisions to use, capacity, operations, tenant value, customer experience and lifecycle cost.

## 3.6 Hook directions

- “Before drawing starts, these decisions matter most.”
- “A beautiful plan can still fail the way you live.”
- “What your architect needs before the first concept.”
- “Why the cheapest quote is not always the lowest project cost.”
- “The approval process clients usually discover too late.”
- “How this site changed the design.”
- “What happens between the sketch and the building site.”
- “The difference between a render and a resolved project.”

## 3.7 Proof assets

- Verified professional registration
- Clear service scope
- Built-project photography
- Drawings and diagrams with client permission
- Design rationale
- Site and context analysis
- Construction-stage documentation
- Project process timeline
- Approved client testimonial
- Budget and scope-control method
- Consultant coordination examples
- Before-and-after renovation evidence
- Post-occupancy feedback
- Procurement or tender process explanation

## 3.8 Offers and lead magnets

- Project-fit consultation
- Feasibility discussion
- Site and brief checklist
- Renovation planning checklist
- “What to prepare before appointing an architect” guide
- Development feasibility intake
- Space-planning consultation
- Approval-process guide
- Professional-fee explanation
- Project-stage roadmap
- Existing-building assessment
- Commercial space brief template

## 3.9 Content formats

- Project story
- Site analysis
- Design decision explainer
- Plan walkthrough
- Construction progress
- Before-and-after renovation
- Material choice explanation
- Budget and scope lesson
- Approval-process explainer
- Myth versus fact
- Client brief transformation
- Professional-role explainer
- Post-occupancy lesson
- Problem-solution case study

## 3.10 Landing-page structure

1. Identify the project type.
2. Explain who the service is for.
3. Clarify the process and project stages.
4. Show relevant project evidence.
5. State professional credentials and service boundaries.
6. Explain what affects fees, timeline and scope.
7. Answer approvals, budget, consultant and construction questions.
8. Use one action: project-fit enquiry, consultation or brief submission.

## 3.11 Objection handling

- “Professional fees are too high” → explain scope, risk reduction, documentation and decision value.
- “A builder can draw it” → explain professional responsibility and project requirements without insulting contractors.
- “I just need plans” → clarify the difference between drawings, approvals and a resolved project.
- “Can you guarantee approval?” → state clearly that approval depends on the authority and compliant submission.
- “Can you guarantee the construction cost?” → explain estimate limits and unknown conditions.
- “I already know what I want” → show how the brief is tested and resolved.
- “I need it quickly” → explain realistic stages and dependencies.

## 3.12 Retargeting stages

- Project education
- Process explanation
- Relevant portfolio evidence
- Professional trust
- Consultation preparation
- Enquiry completion
- Long-cycle nurture
- Project-stage follow-up
- Referral and post-project relationship

## 3.13 Meaningful metrics

- Qualified project enquiries
- Project-fit rate
- Consultation bookings
- Brief completion rate
- Consultation-to-proposal conversion
- Proposal-to-appointment conversion
- Average sales-cycle length
- Lead source by project type
- Cost per qualified project lead
- Referral contribution
- Website actions tied to consultation or brief submission
- Pipeline value where the business tracks it accurately

Do not report clicks, saves or render views as appointed projects.

# 4. Cross-industry operating rules

## Evidence

- Keep official evidence, client facts, client opinions, hypotheses and creative proposals separate.
- Quantified claims require a traceable basis.
- Do not invent stock, capacity, qualifications, approvals, timelines, savings, bookings or project outcomes.
- Testimonials require permission.
- Regulated or safety-related claims require current review.

## Campaign record

Each campaign should capture:

- buyer role;
- pain or trigger;
- desired business action;
- offer;
- proof required;
- format and platform;
- landing destination;
- follow-up path;
- success metric;
- claims and compliance review.

## Candidate Skill Card rule

Do not create one broad “industry expert” card. Create narrow, reviewable candidates for:

- buyer-role selection;
- pain and trigger mapping;
- proof planning;
- offer selection;
- landing-page structure;
- objection handling;
- retargeting sequence;
- metric selection;
- regulated-claim guardrails.

Every candidate must default to `needs_review`.
