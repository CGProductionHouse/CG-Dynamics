## 2. Human brand voice

AV Event Life should sound like a real production team that understands what has to happen before the audience sees the finished event.

Use:
- human, specific wording;
- credible production detail;
- calm competence;
- event-aware energy when the content supports it;
- technical terms only when they help the buyer understand the job;
- real setup/process/crew moments;
- commercial clarity when a post needs bookings or enquiries.

Avoid:
- generic luxury-event language;
- vague hype;
- unsupported scale/bragging;
- technical jargon for its own sake;
- wording that could belong to any event supplier.

The strongest AV Event Life story is usually not `look how amazing the event is`. It is what the team had to set up, test, coordinate or operate to make the public moment work.

## 3. Captions

### Core rule

**Add; do not repeat.**

Read the supplied image, poster or video first. Do not rewrite the headline or dialogue underneath it.

Instead add one useful second layer such as:
- what the setup solved;
- a visible production detail;
- the difference between an empty room and the final event;
- what guests see versus what crew prepared;
- a soundcheck/cue/lighting/stage/process detail;
- a real human crew moment;
- a reason to enquire about the exact service shown.

### Length and rhythm

No complete historical AV Event Life caption corpus was accessible in this migration, so there is no proven fixed sentence count or option count.

Default:
- keep simple posts concise;
- focus on one clear idea;
- vary openings and sentence length;
- expand only for a useful technical explanation, event recap or case-study context;
- do not turn every caption into a service list.

### Language

No durable English/Afrikaans mixing rule was proven.

Use the language of the current brief/artwork naturally. Afrikaans is fine when the supplied context calls for it, but do not force language mixing or slang to manufacture personality.

### Humour

Backstage or crew humour may work when it comes from a real event/setup observation. Keep corporate, safety, failure-risk and serious service communication controlled and credible.

Do not joke by implying unsafe rigging, equipment failure, unreliable service or fake chaos.

### Emojis

No client-specific emoji rule was proven. Use sparingly and functionally, not as a substitute for copy.

## 6. Content that suits AV Event Life

### Venue transformation

Show real progress:
- empty/unfinished venue;
- load-in;
- setup;
- soundcheck/lighting/visual preparation;
- final room/stage;
- live result.

Add one real reason the transformation mattered.

### What guests see vs what crew sees

Useful proof can include:
- setup sequence;
- cables;
- testing;
- cueing;
- lighting programming;
- operator view;
- stage preparation;
- pack-down/load-out.

Only describe what is actually shown or confirmed.

### One problem, one production decision

Strong topics when the exact job supports them:
- speech clarity;
- screen/presentation visibility;
- lighting for the room/stage;
- ceremony or reception audio;
- stage setup;
- livestream/video capture;
- event changeovers;
- technical setup logistics.

Keep explanations accessible and do not drift into unsupported engineering claims.

### Crew proof

Real technicians and operators are stronger than generic `passion` claims. Show competent work, calm preparation and the details guests normally never see.

### Event recaps

When facts and permissions are supplied, a recap can use:
- event type;
- venue/location;
- the requirement;
- AV Event Life's verified contribution;
- one useful setup/process detail;
- visible result/atmosphere.

Do not invent audience numbers, client names, partner status, technical specifications or equipment brands.

## 7. Posters and text-on-post

Keep the idea specific to the visual and service.

Good poster copy usually does one job:
- names a real event problem;
- explains the practical role of the shown service;
- highlights one visible setup detail;
- gives a clean booking/service prompt.

Do not:
- write generic `make it unforgettable` copy;
- paste a long service list over an event image;
- use a stock/AI visual as proof of real capability;
- repeat the poster headline again in the caption.

## 8. Reels and video

Prioritise real production movement:
- venue before/after;
- load-in/build progression;
- soundcheck;
- lighting changes;
- stage reveal;
- technical/operator view;
- real audience reaction where permission exists;
- clear on-screen context.

Useful short-form structure:
1. open on the consequence or visual change;
2. show the setup/process;
3. add one real production detail;
4. reveal the live result;
5. use the exact service/enquiry CTA if the reel is promotional.

Do not invent a technical crisis for drama.
