# Storytelling and Scriptwriting Craft for Short-Form Marketing

Last reviewed: 2026-07-26
Status: Research source pack; review-gated
Primary use: 30-second to 3-minute branded video, social-native short-form, campaign films, testimonials, case studies, event content and staff-led storytelling.

## Purpose

This pack restores foundational pre-AI storytelling craft inside the CG Dynamics Marketing Library.

It is designed to stop short-form work from becoming:

- a list of facts;
- disconnected B-roll;
- a loud hook with no payoff;
- a montage with no human tension;
- generic AI copy;
- or a CTA attached to content that never earned attention.

The operating idea is simple:

> Even a one-minute marketing video should feel like something changed.

A usable story therefore needs:

1. a starting state;
2. a desire, problem or tension;
3. an attempt, development or escalation;
4. a turning point or reveal;
5. a changed end state;
6. a relevant brand or action connection.

## Foundational findings

## 1. Plot is change organised through causality

Aristotle places plot above spectacle and treats action as the core organising principle.

Safe derived rule:

- A story is not merely a sequence of shots.
- The viewer should understand why one moment leads to the next.
- The end should feel caused by what happened before it.

Unsafe overclaim:

- Every marketing video must imitate classical tragedy.

Practical CG test:

Ask:

- What changes?
- Why does it change?
- Can the viewer identify the cause?

Weak:

> Staff arrive. Food is plated. People laugh. Logo appears.

Stronger:

> A group needs an easy place to reconnect. They arrive uncertain, the venue removes the planning friction, the table comes alive, and the evening becomes the outcome they wanted.

## 2. Beginning, middle and end are functional states

A beginning establishes the conditions needed to understand the action.
A middle develops or complicates the action.
An end resolves the central dramatic question or reveals the changed state.

For short-form marketing:

### Beginning

- establish person, situation or tension quickly;
- create an open question;
- make the viewer understand why the next moment matters.

### Middle

- show attempts, friction, proof, escalation or discovery;
- do not repeat the opening in different words;
- move toward a visible change.

### End

- deliver the payoff;
- show the new state;
- connect the outcome to the brand or next action without undoing the emotional finish.

## 3. Unity of action

A strong short-form story should revolve around one central dramatic question.

Examples:

- Will the team pull off the challenge?
- Can this old part be restored?
- What really happens before the customer receives the product?
- Can the staff member solve the customer's problem?
- Why did this client choose this option?
- What changes when the event finally starts?

Everything that does not help answer the central question should be challenged in the edit.

## 4. Reversal, recognition and reveal

Aristotle distinguishes structural moments where action changes direction and where a character or audience understands something important.

Short-form adaptation:

- reversal: the expected outcome changes;
- recognition: the person understands the real problem or solution;
- reveal: the audience receives withheld but relevant information.

Examples:

- The cheap repair becomes the expensive failure.
- The quiet staff member turns out to be the expert.
- The audience expects a standard cocktail, then sees the local twist.
- The customer thinks the problem is price, then discovers the cost of downtime.

These devices should not become dishonest clickbait. The reveal must be supported by the actual footage and facts.

## 5. Stakes

Stakes answer:

> Why does this matter now?

Marketing stakes can be:

- emotional;
- social;
- practical;
- financial;
- reputational;
- time-based;
- identity-based.

Examples:

- Emotional: avoiding embarrassment when hosting a function.
- Social: earning bragging rights in a league.
- Practical: getting the correct part before production stops.
- Financial: avoiding rework.
- Reputational: choosing a supplier who will not fail procurement.
- Time-based: booking before a real deadline.
- Identity-based: feeling like part of a community.

Never invent urgency or scarcity.

## 6. Character desire

A character becomes easier to follow when the viewer can understand what they want.

For branded short-form, the central character may be:

- a customer;
- a staff member;
- a team captain;
- a founder;
- a product in a transformation structure;
- a venue preparing for an event;
- or the viewer themselves through second-person framing.

The desire should be concrete.

Weak:

> She wants a better experience.

Stronger:

> She wants to host twenty colleagues without spending the whole evening fixing problems.

## 7. Conflict is not only confrontation

Conflict is any force that makes the desired outcome uncertain.

Useful short-form conflict types:

- person versus task;
- person versus time;
- person versus misunderstanding;
- person versus habit;
- person versus system;
- person versus environment;
- expectation versus reality;
- old state versus desired state.

Conflict does not require fake arguments, humiliation or manufactured drama.

## 8. Causal compression

A one-minute story cannot show everything.
It must preserve the causal chain while removing non-essential steps.

A compressed structure:

```text
Context
→ disruption or desire
→ attempt
→ complication or proof
→ turning point
→ result
```

The editor may compress time, but must not fabricate causality.

## Short-form story architectures

## A. Setup → tension → payoff

Best for:

- challenges;
- staff humour;
- event moments;
- product reveals;
- before-and-after content.

Template:

1. Show the normal state.
2. Introduce the problem, bet, challenge or unanswered question.
3. Escalate through action.
4. Deliver the result.
5. End on reaction, consequence or brand connection.

## B. Problem → failed assumption → real solution

Best for:

- educational ads;
- technical industries;
- misconception content;
- expert-led videos.

Template:

1. State the common belief.
2. Show why it fails or is incomplete.
3. Reveal the overlooked factor.
4. Demonstrate the better approach.
5. Give the relevant next step.

## C. Before → trigger → after

Best for:

- transformations;
- renovations;
- repairs;
- hospitality setup;
- service outcomes.

Required truth:

- before and after must be comparable;
- the trigger must be shown accurately;
- do not attribute the full outcome to the advertised service when other material factors contributed.

## D. Desire → obstacle → attempt → outcome

Best for:

- customer stories;
- founder stories;
- community sport;
- team-led narratives.

This is the clearest general-purpose dramatic arc for one-minute branded stories.

## E. Mystery → clues → reveal

Best for:

- craftsmanship;
- product detail;
- venue reveals;
- identity-led campaigns.

Safety:

- do not hide essential commercial conditions;
- do not imply a false scandal, danger or result merely to create curiosity.

## F. Countdown or deadline

Best for:

- event preparation;
- launch day;
- production deadlines;
- challenge completion.

The deadline must be real.

## G. Test → evidence → verdict

Best for:

- product demonstrations;
- comparisons;
- service proof;
- technical content.

Required evidence:

- defined test condition;
- fair comparison;
- visible outcome;
- limitations;
- no universal superiority claim from one demonstration.

## One-minute storytelling timing models

These are planning guides, not rigid laws.

## 60-second model

- 0–5 sec: situation and open question
- 5–15 sec: desire/problem and stakes
- 15–35 sec: attempts, proof or escalation
- 35–50 sec: turning point and result
- 50–60 sec: emotional finish, brand meaning or CTA

## 30-second model

- 0–3 sec: specific situation
- 3–10 sec: tension
- 10–22 sec: proof/action
- 22–27 sec: payoff
- 27–30 sec: next step

## 15-second model

- one situation;
- one tension;
- one proof moment;
- one result;
- one action.

Do not force a full multi-character arc into fifteen seconds when a demonstration or single beat is more appropriate.

## Scene design

Every scene or beat should perform at least one job:

- reveal character;
- advance action;
- increase stakes;
- supply proof;
- create contrast;
- change understanding;
- set up or pay off information;
- move toward the CTA.

A scene that performs none of these jobs is a removal candidate.

## The scene question

Before writing a scene, define:

- Who wants what?
- What blocks them?
- What changes by the end?
- What must the viewer understand?
- What can be shown instead of explained?

## Enter late, leave early

For short-form:

- start as close as possible to the meaningful action;
- remove greetings, setup chatter and repeated context;
- end after the payoff rather than explaining the payoff again.

This principle must not remove information required for truth, safety, pricing or conditions.

## Visual storytelling

Prefer visible action over explanatory narration where possible.

Instead of:

> “Our team checks every order carefully.”

Show:

- the checklist;
- the measurement;
- the staff member catching a mismatch;
- the corrected order leaving the floor.

Visual proof should be readable and genuine, not staged to imply a process that does not exist.

## Dialogue craft

Dialogue should sound like a person pursuing an objective, not a brochure being read aloud.

Useful rules:

- give each speaker a reason to speak;
- allow contrast between speakers;
- remove greetings and filler unless character depends on them;
- avoid explaining what the image already proves;
- preserve natural local language and rhythm;
- keep legal, technical and pricing claims exact;
- never invent a testimonial or customer quote.

## Subtext

Subtext is the meaning beneath the literal words.

Example:

Literal:
> “Is that all the stock we have?”

Subtext:
> The deadline may be in danger.

Subtext can make short-form dialogue feel human, but should not create deceptive implications.

## Voice-over

Use voice-over when it adds:

- context unavailable visually;
- internal thought;
- compressed time;
- contrast between expectation and reality;
- or a clean narrative spine.

Avoid voice-over that simply describes every visible action.

## Setup and payoff

A setup plants information.
A payoff uses or resolves it later.

Short-form examples:

- Setup: captain confidently predicts an easy win.
- Payoff: final reaction after the opposite result.

- Setup: staff member says the repair will take five minutes.
- Payoff: the hidden problem is revealed.

- Setup: customer asks whether the venue can handle the group.
- Payoff: reveal the completed setup and atmosphere.

A payoff should be understandable without requiring the viewer to remember too many details.

## Contrast

Contrast helps one-minute stories communicate quickly.

Useful contrasts:

- before versus after;
- expectation versus reality;
- novice versus expert;
- quiet preparation versus loud event;
- wrong approach versus right approach;
- empty venue versus full atmosphere;
- product detail versus full application;
- confidence versus consequence.

Do not fake the weaker state or unfairly misrepresent a competitor.

## Emotional arc

A story may move through emotional states such as:

- uncertainty → relief;
- confidence → surprise;
- frustration → competence;
- isolation → belonging;
- anticipation → celebration;
- scepticism → trust;
- pressure → release.

Research using large story corpora has identified recurring emotional-trajectory shapes, but these findings do not prove that six shapes explain every story or guarantee popularity.

CG use:

- define intended opening emotion;
- define intended closing emotion;
- ensure the middle contains the event that causes the change.

## Brand integration

The brand should be part of the causal story, proof or world—not a logo pasted onto the end.

Possible roles:

- enabler;
- guide;
- setting;
- tool;
- proof source;
- community organiser;
- trusted expert.

Unsafe role:

- magical hero that receives all credit regardless of the evidence.

## CTA after payoff

A CTA should follow naturally from the resolved story.

Examples:

- “Book your team’s table.”
- “Send the application details for a fit check.”
- “See the full process.”
- “Request the procurement pack.”
- “Join the next fixture.”

Do not interrupt the turning point with a premature sales line.

## Beat-sheet template

```text
1. Opening image / starting state
2. Inciting moment / reason this starts now
3. Desire or objective
4. First attempt
5. Escalation, complication or proof
6. Turning point / reveal
7. Outcome
8. Meaning / brand connection
9. CTA
```

Not every script needs all nine beats. The writer should preserve the smallest number needed for a complete causal change.

## One-minute script example — venue function

Objective:
Generate qualified group-function enquiries.

Opening state:
An organiser is overwhelmed by messages, dietary questions and setup decisions.

Dramatic question:
Can the function feel effortless for the organiser?

Beat 1:
Close-up of chaotic WhatsApp messages and checklist.

Beat 2:
Organiser enters venue carrying decorations and looking stressed.

Beat 3:
Staff member takes the box and says, “You enjoy the people. We’ll handle the room.”

Beat 4:
Fast sequence of table setup, food, signage and final checks.

Beat 5:
Doors open; guests arrive.

Turning point:
Organiser puts the phone away and joins the table.

Outcome:
She is laughing instead of coordinating.

CTA:
“Planning a team function? Send us your group size and date.”

Truth requirements:

- only show services genuinely offered;
- do not imply all décor or setup is included unless confirmed;
- show exact booking conditions separately where needed.

## One-minute script example — industrial supplier

Opening state:
A machine stops because a replacement component does not fit the actual application.

Dramatic question:
Is matching the visible size enough?

Beat 1:
Technician holds two nearly identical components.

Beat 2:
“One fits the drawing. One fits the job.”

Beat 3:
Show load, environment and tolerance checks.

Beat 4:
Reveal the hidden difference.

Turning point:
Correct component is installed and the machine restarts.

Outcome:
Downtime ends because application details were checked.

CTA:
“Send the application, load and environment for a fit review.”

Truth requirements:

- use a real or clearly labelled demonstration;
- no universal performance claim;
- do not expose confidential customer equipment.

## Story diagnosis

When a video feels flat, diagnose the missing element.

### No clear beginning

Symptoms:

- viewer cannot identify situation;
- opening contains contextless B-roll;
- first line is a generic slogan.

Fix:

- establish a person, task, place or problem immediately.

### No tension

Symptoms:

- everything goes perfectly from the first second;
- no uncertainty;
- no reason to keep watching.

Fix:

- identify the real obstacle, question, contrast or risk.

### Repetitive middle

Symptoms:

- multiple shots communicate the same fact;
- no escalation;
- narration repeats itself.

Fix:

- make each beat change information, stakes or progress.

### Weak turning point

Symptoms:

- reveal was already obvious;
- result appears without cause;
- CTA arrives instead of payoff.

Fix:

- show the decisive action or understanding.

### No ending

Symptoms:

- video simply stops;
- logo replaces resolution;
- outcome is not visible.

Fix:

- show the changed state before brand sign-off.

### Brand detached from story

Symptoms:

- story could promote any competitor;
- logo only appears in the final frame;
- brand has no causal role.

Fix:

- connect proof, environment, expertise or community role earlier.

## Review rubric

Score each area from 0–2.

### Story clarity

- starting state understandable;
- central question clear;
- outcome understandable.

### Causality

- beats connect logically;
- turning point causes or explains result;
- no fabricated attribution.

### Character and stakes

- desire is concrete;
- obstacle is visible;
- stakes are relevant.

### Compression

- no repeated beats;
- context is sufficient;
- ending arrives before attention is exhausted.

### Visual storytelling

- important proof is shown;
- dialogue does not duplicate image;
- visual sequence remains readable without audio where practical.

### Brand and action

- brand role is meaningful;
- CTA follows the payoff;
- next step matches audience intent.

### Truth and rights

- facts verified;
- music licensed;
- participants authorised;
- conditions retained;
- no confidential material exposed.

## Candidate Skill Cards

### Story Spine for One-Minute Marketing Video

Claim:
A complete short-form story should establish a starting state, create a central question, develop through causal beats, reach a turning point and show a changed ending.

Limitations:
Not every useful video requires a dramatic story. Direct demonstration, announcement and reference content may use simpler structures.

### Desire–Obstacle–Outcome

Claim:
Defining what the central person wants, what blocks them and what changes produces a practical story spine for many branded videos.

Limitations:
The brand must not fabricate conflict or claim sole causation without evidence.

### Enter Late, Leave Early

Claim:
Short-form scenes usually improve when they begin near the meaningful action and end after the payoff rather than including routine setup and repeated explanation.

Limitations:
Do not remove disclosures, conditions or safety context required for truth.

### Show Proof, Not Decorative B-Roll

Claim:
Visual evidence should advance the story or substantiate the proposition rather than merely decorate narration.

Limitations:
A single demonstration does not prove universal performance.

### CTA After Resolution

Claim:
The CTA generally works better structurally when it follows the payoff and extends the viewer’s next logical action.

Limitations:
This is a craft principle, not a guaranteed performance result.

## Implementation boundaries

- The AI may generate drafts, beat sheets and alternatives.
- It may not invent client facts, dialogue presented as real, testimonials, outcomes or urgency.
- It must retrieve active client knowledge before writing client-specific scripts.
- It must identify claims and proof still required.
- It must keep historical/public-domain theory separate from current platform rules.
- It must not expose raw footage or client-internal notes.
- Every production-ready script requires human review.
