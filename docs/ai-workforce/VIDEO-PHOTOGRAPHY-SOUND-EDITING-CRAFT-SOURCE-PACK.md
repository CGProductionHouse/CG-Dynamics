# AI Workforce — Video, Photography, Sound and Editing Craft Source Pack

Last updated: 2026-07-26
Status: Research complete for human review. All candidate knowledge remains review-gated.

## Purpose

This pack gives CG Production House a practical, evidence-aware production system for creating stronger marketing video, photography and sound.

It is not a generic film-school textbook. It is designed to help staff and AI agents:

- translate a marketing objective into a shootable concept;
- choose the right shots, sound and edit structure;
- retain brand attribution while earning attention;
- create platform-ready masters and cutdowns;
- improve accessibility and comprehension;
- separate technical quality from marketing effectiveness;
- review work consistently without pretending that one style fits every client.

## Operating model

```text
Business objective
→ audience and buying situation
→ proposition and proof
→ concept
→ script and storyboard
→ shot and sound plan
→ production
→ edit and finishing
→ platform adaptations
→ quality review
→ publication
→ measured outcome
```

A technically beautiful video can still fail if the message, proof, brand or CTA is weak.
A rough, native-looking video can succeed when it is relevant and credible.
Neither outcome should be assumed in advance.

# 1. Source register

## 1.1 W3C Web Accessibility Initiative — Making Audio and Video Media Accessible

Canonical source:
- https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/

Authority:
- W3C Web Accessibility Initiative.

Rights state:
- Public guidance may be summarised and cited. Do not reproduce large sections verbatim.

Core findings:

- Accessibility should be planned during scripting, storyboarding and production rather than added only at export.
- Captions, transcripts and description of important visual information solve different access problems.
- Integrated description is often easier when considered before filming.
- Media-player support is part of the final experience.

Practical CG rule:

```text
Plan accessibility before filming.
Do not assume platform auto-captions complete the job.
```

Limitations:
- W3C guidance explains accessibility, not ad effectiveness.
- Exact legal obligations depend on jurisdiction and context.

## 1.2 W3C — Captions/Subtitles

Canonical source:
- https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/captions/

Core findings:

- Captions represent spoken words, speaker identity when needed and important non-speech sounds.
- Captions must be synchronized with the video.
- Pre-recorded synchronized video with meaningful audio requires captions for WCAG Level A contexts.
- Automatic captions usually require human correction.

Practical CG rule:

- Caption the actual meaning, not only the spoken words.
- Check names, Afrikaans words, local accents, brand terms and technical terminology manually.
- Important sounds such as `[alarm sounds]`, `[crowd cheers]` or `[machine starts]` should be represented when relevant.

## 1.3 W3C — Audio and Video Content

Canonical source:
- https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/av-content/

Core findings:

- Record clear speech.
- Keep background audio low enough that speech remains understandable.
- Use sufficient contrast for on-screen text.
- Allow viewers enough time to process information.
- Important visual information should also be communicated through audio or description where needed.

Practical CG rule:

```text
Dialogue intelligibility outranks decorative music.
```

## 1.4 YouTube Help — Automatic captioning

Canonical source:
- https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6373554

Core findings:

- YouTube automatic captions are generated by machine learning and can misrepresent speech.
- Accents, dialects, background noise, overlapping speakers and poor sound reduce accuracy.
- YouTube explicitly recommends reviewing and editing automatic captions.

Practical CG rule:

- Never publish client-critical names, offers, legal claims or technical content without reviewing auto-captions.

Limitations:
- This is YouTube-specific platform guidance.

## 1.5 YouTube / Google Ads — ABCDs of effective video ads

Canonical source:
- https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/14783551

Framework:

- Attention
- Branding
- Connection
- Direction

Core findings:

- Reach the heart of the story quickly.
- Use engaging pacing and framing.
- Reinforce the story with audio and text.
- Branding and direction should be deliberately built into the ad.

Google reports potential lifts associated with the framework, but those figures come from Google’s own analysis and must not be treated as a universal guarantee.

Practical CG rule:

```text
Attention without brand attribution is wasted attention.
Branding without human relevance is ignored branding.
```

## 1.6 Google Ads — Creative guidance

Canonical source:
- https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/13812351

Current detectable attributes include:

- a prominent brand logo in the first five seconds;
- duration aligned with objective;
- high-quality human voice-over;
- multiple orientations including horizontal, vertical and square.

Practical CG use:

- Treat these as platform optimisation signals, not mandatory laws for every organic video.
- Use Google’s retention curves and moments analysis when available.

Limitations:
- Google guidance is optimised for Google inventory.
- A detected attribute does not prove a creative idea is persuasive.

## 1.7 Google Ads — Video formats and specifications

Canonical sources:
- https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/2375464
- https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/17091270

Current format implications:

- YouTube uses skippable, non-skippable, bumper, in-feed, Shorts and Masthead formats.
- Recommended lengths differ by placement and objective.
- Vertical, horizontal and square masters serve different inventory.
- Shorts ads are commonly recommended below 60 seconds.
- Format requirements can change and must be checked before delivery.

Practical CG rule:

- Do not crop one horizontal master blindly into every platform.
- Plan protected framing and text-safe zones during the shoot.

## 1.8 Google Ads — Video analytics

Canonical source:
- https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6086441

- Audience retention can be reviewed by creative moment.
- Brand logo, product image and other moments can be marked and compared with retention.

Practical CG rule:

Use retention to locate attention loss.
Do not call retention proof of persuasion, lead quality or sales.

# 2. Marketing-first pre-production

## 2.1 Define the job before the format

Every production brief must answer:

1. What business outcome is this meant to support?
2. What audience or buying group matters?
3. What situation makes the message relevant now?
4. What should the viewer understand, feel or do?
5. What proof can be filmed or shown?
6. Which platform and placement will carry it?
7. What is the primary metric?
8. What claim or rights restrictions apply?

Weak instruction:

> Make a cool 30-second video.

Better instruction:

> Show local restaurant groups that the venue can host relaxed year-end functions, using real group atmosphere, food, private-space proof and a booking CTA. Produce 9:16 and 1:1 versions. Primary outcome: qualified function enquiries.

## 2.2 Select one communication priority

A single asset may contain multiple facts, but it needs one dominant communication job.

Candidate jobs:

- introduce;
- explain;
- demonstrate;
- prove;
- compare;
- reassure;
- invite;
- convert;
- retain;
- reactivate.

Do not force awareness, education, full product detail, testimonial, pricing and lead capture into one short video.

## 2.3 Proposition and proof

Store separately:

```text
Proposition: what the viewer should believe
Proof: what makes that belief credible
```

Proof assets can include:

- real product demonstration;
- measurable result with method;
- customer testimony with authority;
- qualified expert explanation;
- process footage;
- before-and-after with comparable conditions;
- certification or registration with current evidence;
- inventory, location or team capability;
- transparent terms.

A cinematic shot is not proof by itself.

## 2.4 Concept test before shooting

Score the concept from 1–5 on:

- relevance;
- clarity;
- brand attribution;
- proof;
- emotional or human value;
- platform fit;
- feasibility;
- rights and compliance safety;
- repeatability;
- CTA continuity.

Do not proceed because the concept merely sounds visually exciting.

# 3. Script and story structure

## 3.1 Short-form structure

Useful starting pattern:

```text
0–2 sec: relevant interruption or recognisable situation
2–6 sec: establish problem, tension or promise
6–15 sec: demonstrate, explain or reveal
15–25 sec: proof and brand connection
final beat: specific next action
```

This is a planning template, not a platform law.

## 3.2 Hook types

### Situation hook

Show a recognisable moment immediately.

Example:
> The machine stops during the busiest shift.

### Outcome hook

Lead with the desired future state.

Example:
> Eight content pieces from one organised shoot day.

### Contradiction hook

Challenge a common assumption.

Example:
> More views did not mean more bookings.

### Demonstration hook

Begin with the product or result in action.

### Identity hook

Speak to who the viewer is or wants to be.

### Question hook

Use only when the question is specific enough to earn attention.

Weak:
> Did you know?

Better:
> Why does the same paint colour look different on two walls?

### Social or reaction hook

Use a real human reaction where it advances the message. Do not stage fake surprise as evidence.

## 3.3 Voice and dialogue

- Write for speech, not for a brochure.
- Prefer concrete words and short clauses.
- Let staff speak naturally within verified facts.
- Preserve local language and rhythm without caricature.
- Remove throat-clearing introductions.
- Do not make every person say the brand slogan unnaturally.

## 3.4 On-screen text

On-screen text should:

- reinforce the main message;
- remain legible on mobile;
- stay within safe areas;
- use sufficient contrast;
- remain on screen long enough to read;
- avoid covering faces, products or platform controls;
- match spoken facts and current terms.

Do not use captions as decorative wallpaper.

# 4. Storyboarding and shot planning

## 4.1 Storyboard fields

Each beat should store:

```text
beat_id
purpose
visual
shot size
camera movement
audio/dialogue
on-screen text
brand cue
proof asset
estimated duration
platform crop notes
rights/consent note
```

## 4.2 Coverage model

Minimum useful coverage often includes:

- establishing shot;
- medium action;
- close detail;
- person or human reaction;
- proof shot;
- brand/location cue;
- CTA or final outcome shot.

Coverage should serve the edit. More footage is not automatically better.

## 4.3 Shot-size intent

- Wide: context, scale, environment, group dynamics.
- Medium: action and human interaction.
- Close-up: detail, texture, emotion or proof.
- Insert: object, interface, result or process step.
- Over-shoulder: point of view, demonstration or collaboration.

Shot size should be selected for meaning, not random variety.

## 4.4 Camera movement

Use movement when it adds:

- reveal;
- energy;
- spatial understanding;
- subject tracking;
- emotional proximity.

Avoid movement that only announces the camera operator.

A locked shot is often stronger for:

- expert explanations;
- evidence;
- precise product demonstration;
- calm authority;
- graphics and text integration.

## 4.5 Multi-format protection

Before filming, identify:

- horizontal master area;
- vertical protected area;
- square protected area;
- caption zone;
- CTA zone;
- platform-interface zones.

Frame essential faces, products and text so the crop does not destroy the message.

# 5. Photography craft for marketing

## 5.1 Every image needs a job

Common jobs:

- show product accurately;
- create desire;
- show scale;
- explain use;
- establish trust;
- humanise staff;
- prove atmosphere;
- document process;
- support a specific campaign claim.

## 5.2 Marketing image set

For a product, service or venue, capture a useful system rather than only hero images:

- hero;
- context;
- detail;
- use case;
- human interaction;
- proof;
- brand cue;
- negative-space variant for text;
- vertical and horizontal compositions;
- clean catalogue image where applicable.

## 5.3 Composition

Composition should control:

- visual priority;
- subject-background separation;
- eye direction;
- leading lines;
- balance;
- negative space;
- text placement;
- crop resilience.

Rules such as thirds, symmetry or centred framing are tools, not mandatory formulas.

## 5.4 Product and food truth

- Avoid colour grading that materially misrepresents the product.
- Do not make portions, room sizes, finishes or stock look materially different from reality.
- Record styling interventions that could affect the claim.
- Keep comparable lighting and framing for evidence-oriented before-and-after images.

## 5.5 People and consent

Store:

- who appears;
- role;
- consent basis;
- client approval;
- usage scope;
- expiry or withdrawal notes;
- minor status;
- location restrictions.

Public presence at an event does not automatically create unrestricted advertising permission.

# 6. Lighting

## 6.1 Lighting goals

Lighting should support:

- visibility;
- subject separation;
- mood;
- texture;
- product accuracy;
- brand tone;
- continuity.

## 6.2 Practical lighting check

- Is the face readable?
- Are eyes visible where appropriate?
- Is the key product accurately represented?
- Are highlights clipped?
- Are important dark details lost?
- Does mixed colour temperature create an unwanted cast?
- Does the background compete with the subject?
- Will the grade remain consistent across shots?

## 6.3 Natural light

Natural light is useful but unstable.

Record:

- time of day;
- window direction;
- cloud changes;
- colour-temperature changes;
- exposure continuity.

Do not rely on a fifteen-minute golden-light look for a three-hour interview schedule without a continuity plan.

## 6.4 Brand versus realism

Not every client needs glossy commercial lighting.

Possible modes:

- clean authority;
- warm hospitality;
- raw social-native;
- premium product;
- documentary process;
- high-energy event;
- technical demonstration.

The mode must match the strategy and audience.

# 7. Sound recording and design

## 7.1 Dialogue priority

Clear dialogue generally matters more than expensive camera bodies for explanation-led marketing.

Record:

- primary microphone;
- backup microphone where risk warrants it;
- room tone;
- ambient sound;
- clean product/process sound;
- music-rights source;
- noise issues and corrective notes.

## 7.2 Microphone placement

Select based on:

- speaker movement;
- clothing noise;
- room reflections;
- background noise;
- camera distance;
- concealment needs;
- backup options.

Do not assume a lavalier automatically beats a well-positioned boom or directional microphone.

## 7.3 Monitor during recording

Check for:

- clipping;
- interference;
- cable noise;
- clothing rustle;
- air-conditioning;
- traffic;
- refrigerators;
- music in the venue;
- overlapping speakers;
- room echo.

A waveform on screen does not prove intelligibility.

## 7.4 Music

Music selection must pass the separate rights system:

`MUSIC-COPYRIGHT-PLATFORM-RIGHTS-SYSTEM.md`

Also evaluate:

- emotional fit;
- pace;
- brand fit;
- edit points;
- dialogue competition;
- duration;
- platform/account licence;
- paid-use rights;
- client-project registration;
- certificate storage.

“Copyright-free” is not a sufficient licence state.

## 7.5 Sound design

Useful sound design may include:

- real process sounds;
- transitions motivated by action;
- room atmosphere;
- crowd energy;
- tactile product detail;
- subtle emphasis.

Avoid over-design that makes authentic footage feel fake or distracts from the message.

# 8. Editing

## 8.1 Edit for meaning first

First pass:

- establish the argument or story;
- remove repetition;
- retain the strongest proof;
- make the brand identifiable;
- preserve human credibility;
- lead to one action.

Only then optimise transitions, effects and polish.

## 8.2 Pacing

Pacing depends on:

- message complexity;
- audience familiarity;
- platform;
- emotional tone;
- shot information density;
- dialogue speed;
- text-reading time.

Fast cuts are not automatically engaging.
Slow pacing is not automatically premium.

## 8.3 Continuity

Check:

- action continuity;
- eyeline;
- screen direction;
- wardrobe and prop position;
- lighting;
- product state;
- ambient sound;
- factual continuity;
- time and location logic.

## 8.4 B-roll discipline

B-roll should:

- show what is being discussed;
- provide evidence;
- hide necessary cuts;
- add context;
- maintain interest.

Avoid generic B-roll that contradicts the speaker or replaces necessary proof.

## 8.5 Graphics and motion

Graphics should explain, identify or direct.

Use them for:

- names and roles;
- prices and terms;
- steps;
- dimensions;
- comparisons;
- maps;
- results;
- CTA;
- brand cues.

Every numeric claim needs evidence and scope.

## 8.6 Colour

Colour finishing should:

- maintain skin-tone credibility;
- protect product colour truth;
- match shots;
- support brand mood;
- remain legible on mobile;
- avoid crushed or clipped information.

A heavy preset is not a grade.

## 8.7 Captions

Caption workflow:

1. Generate or transcribe.
2. Correct words and names.
3. Correct punctuation and segmentation.
4. Add relevant non-speech information.
5. Check timing.
6. Check safe zones and contrast.
7. Export platform-appropriate burned-in and/or sidecar versions.
8. Review on a phone with sound off.

Preferred deliverables where applicable:

- clean high-quality master;
- 16:9 version;
- 9:16 version;
- 1:1 or 4:5 version;
- captioned version;
- clean version without burned-in text;
- short cutdowns;
- thumbnail/poster frame;

## 9.2 TikTok and Reels

Common execution needs:

- immediate contextual clarity;
- vertical framing;
- readable text;
- human presence where useful;
- platform-safe music;
- no third-party watermark;
- original footage or authorised assets;
- concise CTA;
- tested duration rather than a universal duration myth.

Use current platform policy sources before posting regulated products, alcohol, gambling, health or financial content.

## 9.3 YouTube

Separate:

- Shorts;
- organic long-form;
- skippable ads;
- bumpers;
- in-feed;
- non-skippable;

## 9.4 LinkedIn

Prioritise:

- professional relevance;
- credible experts;
- real process;
- customer or operational proof;
- strong mobile legibility;
- clear business CTA.

Do not turn every B2B video into a stiff corporate montage.

## 9.5 Facebook and Instagram

Create separate variants where placement behaviour differs.

Check:

- feed crop;
- Reels crop;
- Story safe zones;
- sound-off comprehension;
- music rights;
- commercial disclosure;
- ad-policy claims.

# 10. Creative formats

## 10.1 Expert explanation

Best for:

- complex services;
- regulated topics;
- technical authority;
- objection handling.

Required:

- verified speaker role;
- concise script;
- clean sound;
- proof visuals;
- appropriate disclaimer where necessary.

## 10.2 Demonstration

Best for:

- products;
- process;
- application;
- comparison;
- transformation.

Required:

- representative conditions;
- no hidden manipulation;
- clear result criteria;
- exact claim scope.

## 10.3 Testimonial or case study

Structure:

```text
Situation
→ problem
→ decision
→ implementation
→ verified result
→ limitation/context
```

Do not script a customer into making unsupported universal claims.

## 10.4 Behind the scenes

Useful for:

- trust;
- craftsmanship;
- team character;
- process proof;
- recruitment;
- brand familiarity.

It still needs a point. Random footage of staff working is not automatically a story.

## 10.5 Event recap

Separate:

- atmosphere;
- attendance evidence;
- sponsor proof;
- participant story;
- next-event CTA.

Do not imply total attendance from selected crowded angles.

## 10.6 Reaction-led content

Works when:

- the reaction is real or clearly performed entertainment;
- the source footage is licensed;
- the reaction advances a relevant message;
- commercial and platform restrictions are met.

## 10.7 Offer-led ad

Required fields:

- exact offer;
- eligible audience;
- price;
- inclusions;
- exclusions;
- dates;
- stock or capacity;
- location;
- CTA;
- landing-page consistency.

# 14. Common failure patterns

## Beautiful but anonymous

The work looks premium but could belong to any competitor.

Correction:
- use real distinctive assets, people, places, sounds and proof.

## Loud but irrelevant

The hook earns attention but has little connection to the offer.

Correction:
- link the interruption directly to a buying situation.

## Montage without argument

Multiple attractive shots with no clear message.

Correction:
- define one proposition and order shots around it.

## Dialogue buried by music

Correction:
- rebalance, simplify and prioritise intelligibility.

## One master cropped everywhere

Correction:
- create planned platform versions.

## Auto-captions published unchecked

Correction:
- manually review every meaningful word.

## Generic testimonial

> Great service. Highly recommended.

Correction:
- capture the situation, decision, experience and verified result.

## Views reported as success

Correction:
- connect creative diagnostics to the objective and downstream outcome.

## Trend copied without brand fit

Correction:
- adapt the mechanic to the client’s audience, voice and proof.

# 16. AI agent behaviour

When asked to plan a shoot or video, the agent must:

1. Retrieve exact client knowledge.
2. Retrieve the relevant industry pack.
3. Retrieve current platform and rights knowledge.
4. Confirm objective and outcome state.
5. Identify proposition and proof.
6. Propose a concept and platform variants.
7. Produce a script, storyboard and shot/sound plan.
8. Flag permissions, claims and rights.
9. Define review criteria and measurement.

The agent must not:

- invent client facts;
- invent a testimonial;
- claim a trend guarantees reach;
- provide unsupported technical or regulated claims;
- assume music is licensed;
- expose raw footage or internal OneDrive folders to clients;
- convert views into revenue;
- activate unreviewed Skill Cards.
