## Purpose

National campaign case studies are not enough. Rugby marketing in South Africa must understand the ordinary culture around the game:

- people wearing jerseys to work and school;
- Bok Fridays and green-and-gold office culture;
- braais before and during matches;
- pubs, restaurants and homes becoming informal supporter venues;
- provincial rivalries passed through families;
- school rugby Saturdays;
- club rugby and town derbies;
- radio commentary and supporter language;
- prediction groups and WhatsApp banter;
- local heroes progressing from school and provincial rugby to the Springboks;
- the emotional importance of the Currie Cup;
- the Super 12 and Super 14 era that shaped a generation of supporters;
- Cheetahs rugby as part of Free State identity, not merely another professional team.

The marketing opportunity is not to manufacture culture. It is to recognise existing rituals and give supporters a useful, enjoyable way to participate.

## 1. South African rugby is layered, not one audience

A useful rugby campaign distinguishes between:

1. **Springbok national culture** — green and gold, national pride, Rugby World Cups, the Rugby Championship, test-match weekends and Bok Fridays.
2. **Provincial culture** — Currie Cup heritage, local unions, interprovincial rivalry and town or provincial pride.
3. **Franchise-era culture** — Super 12, Super 14 and later Super Rugby memories, including the Cheetahs entering Super 14 in 2006.
4. **Current professional competitions** — the competitions and teams active at the time of the campaign must be verified from current official schedules.
5. **School and university rugby** — interschools, derbies, youth pathways, Varsity Cup and family support.
6. **Club and community rugby** — local grounds, volunteers, former players, supporter clubs and town identity.
7. **Hospitality culture** — braais, bars, restaurants, fan parks, halftime food, match specials and social viewing.

Do not flatten all of these into generic Springbok content.

## 2. Currie Cup culture

SA Rugby describes the Currie Cup as the oldest provincial rugby competition in the world and associates it with respectful rivalry, provincial passion, ambition, pride and community. This gives the competition cultural value beyond the current size of its television audience.

### Human truths

- Support is inherited through families and provinces.
- A provincial jersey can represent home, language, school memories and local pride.
- Smaller unions and towns often carry powerful underdog stories.
- Players are followed as they move from school and provincial rugby toward higher honours.
- A Currie Cup final can revive old rivalries and dormant supporter habits.
- Supporters remember eras, stadiums, commentators, players and family rituals more than generic tournament slogans.

### Useful content directions

- “The jersey I grew up with” supporter stories.
- Grandparent, parent and child provincial-support portraits.
- Historic fixture memories tied to the upcoming match.
- Local players’ journeys from school or club to provincial rugby.
- Away-day travel stories between neighbouring provinces.
- Provincial food, language and supporter rituals.
- Former-player reactions and practical match analysis.
- “Where were you when…?” archival-memory prompts.
- Current squad content linked carefully to verified historical context.

### Activation directions

- Heritage-jersey nights.
- Provincial-family photo walls.
- Supporter storytelling booths.
- Local-school curtain raisers.
- Former-player meetups.
- Braai and supporter-stand experiences.
- Match-week city or town challenges.
- Provincial prediction leagues with meaningful season-long rewards.

SA Rugby’s 2019 Currie Cup #Reloaded campaign is a useful precedent: it used in-stand braais, party stands, DJs, mascots, local comedians and digital commentary content to strengthen attendance and social engagement. The lesson is not to copy those executions blindly; it is to connect the stadium experience to recognisable South African supporter behaviour.

## 3. Super 12 and Super 14 memory culture

The terminology must be historically accurate.

- Super 12 began in 1996 as the professional SANZAR competition involving leading teams from South Africa, New Zealand and Australia.
- Super 14 began in 2006 when the Western Force and Cheetahs joined.
- “Super 14” is therefore a historical competition-era reference, not a generic name for all current southern-hemisphere franchise rugby.

### Why it matters in marketing

For many South African supporters, the Super 12 and Super 14 period created strong Saturday-morning and afternoon rituals:

- early matches from New Zealand and Australia;
- multiple games across one day;
- familiar cross-border rivalries;
- SuperSport viewing culture;
- fantasy selections and prediction banter;
- distinctive team identities and jerseys;
- road trips and packed home fixtures;
- memories tied to specific generations of players.

### Content opportunities

- “Which Super 14 team did your household support?”
- Historic Cheetahs entry-era memories.
- Supporter stories about first franchise matches.
- Then-versus-now jersey and match-day rituals.
- Former players discussing how the competition felt.
- Archive-informed fixture content with rights-cleared imagery only.
- Nostalgia nights at hospitality venues tied to verified current fixtures.

Do not falsely imply that Super 14 still exists as the current competition. Use it as a cultural and historical reference.

## 4. Springbok culture and Bok Fridays

Bok Friday is a real supporter ritual, not merely a hashtag concept. SA Rugby publicly encouraged supporters to wear green and gold every Friday during the 2019 international season and Rugby World Cup campaign.

### Cultural components

- jerseys at work, school and in shops;
- green-and-gold dress competitions;
- office photos and staff challenges;
- supporter flags on vehicles and buildings;
- braais, breakfast gatherings and match-day food;
- national songs, chants and multilingual supporter messages;
- family viewing traditions;
- community watch parties;
- children dressing like players;
- rugby conversation across workplaces and social groups.

### Marketing uses

- Weekly Bok Friday staff or customer features.
- Local-business participation maps.
- Green-and-gold window or storefront challenges.
- Customer-generated supporter portraits.
- School, workplace and community dress-up moments.
- Match-week menus or bundles that remain truthful and easy to understand.
- Supporter-message walls in multiple local languages.
- Local hero or grassroots rugby fundraising tied to Bok Friday participation.

### Avoid

- using the Springbok name, marks, imagery or player likenesses without the necessary rights;
- pretending a brand is an official partner;
- generic AI-created patriotic speeches;
- exploiting national emotion without giving supporters a real action;
- reducing South African support to one language or demographic.

## 5. Free State and Cheetahs culture

Cheetahs rugby should be treated as part of Free State cultural identity.

### Core context

- Bloemfontein is a central rugby city with long provincial, school and university traditions.
- The Free State Cheetahs have a strong Currie Cup identity.
- The Cheetahs joined Super 14 in 2006, making that era an important part of local supporter memory.
- SA Rugby recorded the Free State Cheetahs’ 2019 Currie Cup victory as their sixth title at that time.
- The Cheetahs won the 2023 Currie Cup and hosted subsequent provincial fixtures in Bloemfontein.
- The Cheetahs’ identity extends into the wider Free State through schools, clubs, towns, families and former players.

### Free State supporter truths

- Local support often combines rugby, braai culture, agriculture, schools, families and town networks.
- Supporters value players who feel accessible and connected to the province.
- Bloemfontein match attendance competes with home braais, restaurants, television and travel distance; the stadium day must therefore feel worth the effort.
- Afrikaans is important but Free State rugby culture is not only Afrikaans. Campaigns should reflect the actual multilingual supporter base.
- Griquas, Griffons and neighbouring provincial rivalries also matter across the central region.
- School rugby pathways and local names can create stronger interest than generic national-player content.

### Cheetahs-specific content engine

#### Heritage

- Currie Cup championship memories.
- Super 14 entry and memorable international opponents.
- Historic jerseys and supporter stories.
- Former Cheetahs and Free State legends.
- Famous home matches and stadium memories.

#### Local pathway

- School to club to academy to senior rugby.
- Coaches, teachers and family members behind a player.
- Small-town Free State player origins.
- Women’s and youth rugby pathways.
- Club and school fixtures connected to the provincial ecosystem.

#### Match week

- Why this opponent matters.
- What supporters should watch tactically.
- Arrival, parking, gates, family facilities and food information.
- Real supporter predictions.
- Local-business and restaurant participation.
- Cheetah Friday or orange-day workplace content where rights and brand approval permit.

#### Human access

- Kit-room, grounds-team and medical-team stories.
- Honest player routines.
- Supporter Q&A sessions.
- Training access.
- Former-player match previews.
- Children meeting local rugby heroes.

### Free State activation ideas

- **Orange Friday:** local workplaces, schools and businesses wear orange before home fixtures, subject to team brand approval.
- **Cheetah Country map:** supporters and clubs across Free State towns submit photos and stories.
- **From our school to the stadium:** trace local player and supporter pathways.
- **Provincial braai table:** local food and supporter stories without turning rugby into a generic braai advert.
- **Town takeover weeks:** selected Free State towns receive player visits, ticket activations and community-club support.
- **Old jersey day:** verified heritage display and supporter portraits.
- **Family stand stories:** multi-generation supporter features.
- **Central derby week:** truthful rivalry content for Griquas, Griffons or other relevant opponents.
- **Cheetahs business network:** local businesses create visible, measurable match-week participation rather than passive logo placement.

## 6. Normal match-day culture that campaigns should capture

Strong rugby content can be built from ordinary details:

- putting meat on the fire before kick-off;
- deciding who brings ice, wood, snacks and drinks;
- children wearing oversized jerseys;
- older family members explaining historic rivalries;
- predicting the score on WhatsApp;
- driving through town with flags;
- meeting at the same pub or house every test;
- singing before and after a match;
- changing seats because someone believes it affects the result;
- the person who refuses to speak during a kick at posts;
- half-time analysis around the braai;
- Monday-morning arguments about referees and selections;
- local radio discussions;
- supporter humour after wins and losses;
- clubhouses, school fields and community halls as rugby spaces.

These details are more useful than generic lines such as “feel the gees”. Show the actual gees.

## 7. Hospitality and local-business applications

Rugby culture can drive restaurant, bar, retail, dealership, tourism and community marketing without every post becoming a fixture poster.

### Content

- the table or venue preparing before supporters arrive;
- staff score predictions;
- regular supporters and their rituals;
- dish or drink preparation linked naturally to match day;
- pre-match arrival and post-match reaction;
- family and group bookings;
- local former-player appearances;
- supporter dress moments;
- safe-transport and booking information.

### Promotions

- clear match-day bundles;
- table-booking benefits;
- verified score-prediction competitions;
- supporter loyalty across a season;
- provincial-colour days;
- community-club fundraising nights;
- sponsor-funded transport or supporter access;
- breakfast offers for early international fixtures.

### Measurement

Track:

- bookings;
- match-day occupancy;
- average spend;
- repeat match attendance;
- promotion redemptions;
- supporter database growth with consent;
- event-specific enquiries;
- no-show rate;
- community-club funds or resources actually delivered.

Do not claim “the home of rugby” or “official fan venue” without evidence and rights.

## 8. Campaign review checklist

Before approving a South African rugby campaign, confirm:

- Is the competition name historically and currently accurate?
- Is the relevant team, union or tournament current for the campaign date?
- Are fixture, venue and broadcast details verified?
- Are team marks, player images, footage and music properly licensed?
- Does the campaign reflect national, provincial, school, club or hospitality culture accurately?
- Is Free State or local identity represented through real people and details rather than stereotypes?
- Is there a meaningful supporter action?
- Is the campaign accessible across the languages and communities actually being addressed?
- Are attendance, sales, voting and engagement outcomes reported separately?
- Does nostalgia support the current objective rather than replace it?

Any future South African sport pack must include:
